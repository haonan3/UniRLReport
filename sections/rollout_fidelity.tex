\section{Case Study II: Rollout Fidelity and Request Shaping}
\label{sec:fidelity}

Whereas Section~\ref{sec:reward} examined how generated trajectories are scored, this case study examines the boundary between the trajectory IR and an external rollout engine.
Even when a rollout adapter returns a structurally well-formed \sample{}, three engine-boundary concerns govern whether optimization updates match the scored behavior policy and how efficiently rollouts execute:
first, the rollout engine and the training role must condition on identical multimodal context representations (Section~\ref{sec:fidelity-layout});
second, the adapter must propagate per-prompt output canvas resolutions while keeping trajectory segments dense (Section~\ref{sec:fidelity-canvas}) and balancing variable-resolution work across data-parallel ranks (Section~\ref{sec:fidelity-tail});
and third, engine requests should pack contiguous sibling rows under a shared prompt so that source conditioning is encoded once per group rather than once per sibling row (Section~\ref{sec:fidelity-pack}).
Verifying and optimizing these properties requires explicit cross-boundary invariants and request-shaping contracts.

\paragraph{Scope.}
This case study examines four stacked refinements to the vLLM-Omni BAGEL rollout path affecting both text-to-image (\texttt{t2i}) and instruction-guided image-to-image editing (\texttt{it2i}) workloads, together with a dynamic load-balancing scheduler for mixed-resolution batches (Section~\ref{sec:fidelity-tail}).
All measurements below were collected on a single node of eight NVIDIA H20 GPUs, with per-run records and table generators archived under \codepath{artifacts/reported\_results/2026-10-04-rollout-fidelity/}.

\subsection{Conditioning Mismatch Undetected by the Importance Ratio}
\label{sec:fidelity-layout}

In instruction-guided image editing (\texttt{it2i}), the policy conditions on a source image alongside the edit instruction.
To guarantee rollout--replay consistency, UniRL's rollout adapter constructs this conditioning using the model bundle's image transforms and vision encoder, injecting the resulting key--value contexts directly into the vLLM-Omni engine so that the training role replays against identical contexts during optimization.
Following an upstream serving-engine migration, however, three helper functions in the BAGEL pipeline continued to read the injected conditioning field from the outer request-batch wrapper after the caller had already unwrapped it into an individual request object.
Because the looked-up attribute was absent on the unwrapped request, the engine silently ignored the injected contexts and fell back to its internal image-to-image preprocessing path---applying a different image transform at a fixed resolution, drawing a sampled rather than posterior-mean VAE latent encoding, and sizing the output canvas to the source image rather than the recipe target.

Despite this bug, training completed without raising an error: every image-editing rollout generated samples under the engine's fallback conditioning (\RFWorkerKv{} context tokens) and replayed them under the trainer's conditioning (\RFTrainerKv{} context tokens at the same $512\times512$ source resolution).
Notably, standard importance-ratio monitoring did not flag the discrepancy.
Before the first optimizer update of a rollout, the current policy $\theta$ equals the behavior policy $\pi_v$, so the importance ratio $\rho$ against the rollout behavior anchor should equal 1 up to floating-point noise.
In practice, the observed ratio remained close to 1 despite the mismatch: the maximum first-update deviation $|\rho - 1|$ was $\RFRatioBuggy\times10^{-5}$ with the conditioning bug versus $\RFRatioHealthy\times10^{-5}$ after the fix, well within the $\RFRatioHealthyEdit\times10^{-5}$ deviation observed in healthy image-editing runs (Table~\ref{tab:fidelity-layout}).
This insensitivity arises from how denoising transition log probabilities are reduced.
Both the rollout engine and the trainer compute the Gaussian log-density of each denoising transition by averaging over all latent elements rather than summing across them; consequently, a perturbation to the predicted velocity mean---even one large enough to alter the generated image---is divided by the number of latent dimensions (tens of thousands of elements at $512\times512$ resolution) before entering the log-probability ratio.
Under element-mean reduction, the importance ratio cannot distinguish a structural conditioning mismatch from benign numerical noise.
Moreover, when a recipe uses the trainer-recomputed replay anchor (the default in several diffusion configurations), the baseline log probabilities are recomputed on the trainer itself, making the first-update ratio identically 1 by construction.

To detect such discrepancies directly, UniRL adds a structural token-layout check across the rollout--replay boundary.
The engine hook that injects initial noise now also records the conditioning layout and output canvas geometry---specifically, the generation-context token length, its positional offset, and the rendered height and width---which return through the capture channel into the frontier layer's model conditions $C_i$ and are compared row-by-row against the context reconstructed by the training role during replay.
Any mismatch raises an exception on the first replay pass and logs both layouts.
Because the training role already constructs the replay context, the check adds no tensor-compute overhead; conditioning paths that pass pre-materialized context tensors directly from rollout to replay bypass the check.
Table~\ref{tab:fidelity-layout} summarizes the fault-injection and regression probes verified by this check.
The importance-ratio check is retained as an optional secondary diagnostic for detecting weight-publication desynchronization or mismatched LoRA adapters, where the model parameters rather than the conditioning layout differ between rollout and replay.

Restoring the injected conditioning affected both rollout latency and initial judge scores.
Generation time for 16 edits per rank decreased from \RFMainGenerate{}\,s to \RFFixGenerate{}\,s (\RFFixSaving{}), because the engine's fallback path encoded the source image into nearly four times as many vision tokens as UniRL's encoder path (\RFWorkerKv{} versus \RFTrainerKv{} total context tokens).
Simultaneously, the mean EditScore reward on the first rollout---evaluated on identical prompts prior to any optimizer update---increased from \RFMainReward{} to \RFFixReward{} (Figure~\ref{fig:fidelity-chain}), reflecting the quality difference between the two conditioning representations on the pretrained checkpoint.

\subsection{Per-Group Output Canvas Resolution}
\label{sec:fidelity-canvas}

Previously, every rollout batch required a single uniform output resolution fixed by the recipe, because a layer's \texttt{LatentSegment} $Z_i$ stores latents as a single dense tensor and the rollout adapter passed one canvas size per batch to the engine.
Production prompt datasets, however, contain examples with diverse target aspect ratios and resolutions; forcing a uniform canvas either wastes compute on low-resolution examples or downsamples and distorts high-resolution ones.

To support variable-resolution datasets without sacrificing dense segment storage, UniRL allows each root example to specify its target canvas dimensions in the root metadata $M_0$.
During rollout, the adapter resolves each frontier row's target canvas from its root ancestor in $P_0$ (falling back to the recipe default) and dispatches one engine call per contiguous run of rows that share a canvas size.
To keep the latent segment tensor dense across a mixed-resolution batch, smaller canvases are right-padded along the token dimension to match the recipe's maximum canvas size, and the trainer slices each row back to its true token count during replay; canvases exceeding the recipe maximum are rejected at admission.
Because all sibling samples forked from a root prompt share the same canvas, group-relative credit assignment, the micro-batched \texttt{RewardStack} of Section~\ref{sec:reward-overlap}, and downstream scorers require no modification.
Datasets without per-example canvas metadata execute identically to the fixed-resolution path.
Rollout backends that support only a single resolution per batch---including the train-side diffusion adapter, non-BAGEL serving adapters, and video pipelines---explicitly reject the per-row canvas metadata field rather than silently rendering at the default resolution.

This padding scheme applies to objectives whose replay anchor stores a single scalar per denoising transition (such as Flow-GRPO).
Objectives that store per-token replay anchors or consume the dense latent field directly still require a uniform canvas across the batch and now fail fast with a descriptive error when given mixed-canvas inputs.

Table~\ref{tab:fidelity-canvas} shows that generation latency scales approximately linearly with the output image-token count, creating a $4\times$ cost difference between $512\times512$ and $1{,}024\times1{,}024$ canvases within the same batch.

\subsection{Sibling Request Packing for Image Editing}
\label{sec:fidelity-pack}

To support group-relative credit assignment, rollout forks multiple sibling samples from each root prompt.
Whereas the text-to-image BAGEL adapter already packed sibling rows into a single engine call, the original image-editing (\texttt{it2i}) adapter dispatched each sibling row as an independent request, causing the engine to recompute the vision-encoder pass and key--value conditioning context for every sibling in the group.
With sibling request packing, whenever guidance-scale settings permit, the \texttt{it2i} adapter consolidates contiguous sibling rows from the same prompt group into a single engine request so that the engine encodes the source conditioning once and broadcasts the resulting context across the group.
At 16 edits per rank ($512\times512$), packing reduces per-rank generation time from \RFCanvasGenerate{}\,s to \RFPackGenerate{}\,s (\RFPackSaving{}, Table~\ref{tab:fidelity-chain}) at the cost of roughly 2\,GiB of additional peak GPU memory, and reduces the generation time for a single eight-sibling group from \RFUnpackedGroupEight{}\,s to \RFPackedGroupEight{}\,s.

Because initial noise tensors are indexed per row, a packed request can span any contiguous subset of a sibling group rather than requiring the full group at once.
Consequently, the adapter no longer requires \texttt{RewardStack} (Section~\ref{sec:reward-overlap}) to round micro-batch sizes up to full group boundaries: a four-row micro-batch from an eight-sibling group executes as a single four-sample packed request.

\subsection{Mitigating Mixed-Resolution Stragglers via Cost-Aware Assignment}
\label{sec:fidelity-tail}

When a rollout batch mixes output canvas resolutions, static data-parallel sharding---assigning prompts in dataset order with equal row counts per rank---creates severe load imbalance across ranks.
On a mixed-resolution dataset whose canvases span a $4\times$ range in image tokens, the default static split left at least one GPU rank idle for \RFTtoiIdle{} of the text-to-image rollout window while waiting for the rank assigned the largest canvases.

To quantify how much of this straggler tail can be recovered without changing trajectory semantics, we evaluated a cost-aware work-assignment prototype.
A driver-side coordinator orders prompt groups by estimated token cost and dispatches work units to ranks from a shared queue; when a rank finishes its assigned queue, work stealing allows it to claim the lowest-cost remaining unit from the busiest rank's queue.
Before credit assignment, completed rows are restored to their canonical parent-contiguous order by sample identifier $I_i$, leaving the trajectory structure consumed by the trainer unchanged.

Table~\ref{tab:fidelity-tail} and the companion two-rollout traces yield three findings:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Static cost balancing eliminates most straggler idle time when the cost model is accurate.} In text-to-image (\texttt{t2i}) generation, cost-balanced assignment reduces combined rollout-plus-reward time from \RFTtoiBase{}\,s to \RFTtoiBalanced{}\,s and cuts the barrier idle fraction from \RFTtoiIdle{} to 2\%, while mean per-rank generation time remains unchanged at approximately 104\,s, confirming that work is redistributed across ranks rather than reduced.
  \item \textbf{Work stealing provides robustness against cost-model misspecification.} Under the image-token cost model, dynamic stealing was never triggered in \texttt{t2i} and transferred at most \RFStealPixelsEditMax{} prompt groups per rollout in \texttt{it2i}. Under a deliberately misspecified cost model that counts only rows and ignores canvas resolution---forfeiting \RFNaiveGap{}\,s of the \texttt{t2i} balancing gain---work stealing recovers \RFStealRecovered{}\,s of that penalty, reducing combined rollout-plus-reward time from \RFNaiveBalanced{}\,s to \RFNaiveSteal{}\,s.
  \item \textbf{Cost-aware assignment composes with generation--reward overlap only when the judge has spare capacity.} In \texttt{it2i} with the 8B EditScore judge, generation--reward overlap alone reduces combined rollout-plus-reward time from \RFSmallBase{}\,s to \RFSmallOverlap{}\,s, cost-balanced assignment with stealing reduces it to \RFSmallSteal{}\,s, and combining both mechanisms reduces it to \RFSmallBoth{}\,s (\RFSmallBothSaving{} total reduction).
\end{enumerate}

By contrast, when paired with the saturated single-replica 72B EditScore judge, cost-aware assignment \emph{increases} combined rollout-plus-reward time relative to the static baseline: in the steady-state rollout, duration rises from \RFLargeBase{}\,s to \RFLargeSteal{}\,s with overlap (and to \RFLargeStealNoOverlap{}\,s without overlap); in the first rollout, it rises from \RFLargeFirstBase{}\,s to \RFLargeFirstSteal{}\,s with overlap (and to \RFLargeFirstStealNoOverlap{}\,s without overlap).
Although cost balancing still reduces the slowest rank's generation time from \RFLargeTail{}\,s to \RFLargeTailSteal{}\,s, equalizing rank completion times causes all eight ranks to submit their final reward requests nearly simultaneously, concentrating the judge queueing delay that the imbalanced static baseline had naturally staggered.
This is the converse of the request-staggering effect analyzed in Section~\ref{sec:reward-results} and implies that generation load balancing should be gated on reward-service headroom (or paired with additional judge replicas and cross-request batching).

A second interaction remains unaddressed in this prototype: in every arm that reordered prompt groups across ranks, the subsequent training phase ran \RFTrainSlowSteadyMin{}--\RFTrainSlowSteadyMax{}\% slower in the steady-state rollout and \RFTrainSlowFirstMin{}--\RFTrainSlowFirstMax{}\% slower in the first rollout.
Control runs that routed dataset-order shards through the same coordinator queue without cross-rank reassignment incurred a \RFTrainSlowOwnerMin{}--\RFTrainSlowOwnerMax{}\% training slowdown, indicating that part of the overhead stems from the queue path itself and the remainder from cross-worker \texttt{TensorRef} transfers when completed rows are restored to canonical parent-contiguous order before training.
While total iteration time in the steady-state rollout remained shorter whenever balancing reduced rollout-plus-reward time, in the first rollout under the 8B judge without overlap the training slowdown outweighed the rollout gain, increasing total iteration time by \RFSmallFirstStepSteal{}\,s.
Propagating the cost-balanced shard assignment directly into the training phase to avoid cross-worker reordering remains future work.
