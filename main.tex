\begin{abstract}
Reinforcement-learning for generative models follows a common semantic loop---generate, score, assign credit, and update---yet existing systems fragment across autoregressive, diffusion, and multi-stage multimodal workflows, as well as across colocated, disaggregated, and asynchronous execution topologies.
We present UniRL, a trajectory-centric system that places a lineage-preserving, modality-aware intermediate representation (IR) at the boundary between post-training semantics and distributed execution.
Each trajectory is represented as a prompt-rooted tree of typed layers, where each generated layer records its decoded primitives, conditioning inputs, stage-specific replay segment, rewards, advantages, and behavior-policy version.
Three logical roles operate on this representation---rollout engines populate generation layers, reward and credit operators evaluate and fold feedback along tree ancestry, and training backends replay stage-specific objectives---while explicit runtime contracts govern device placement, tensor transport, weight publication, and memory residency.
These interfaces decouple model-specific replay from lineage tracking, data movement, and scheduling, without forcing heterogeneous objectives or engine--topology pairs into a monolithic trainer.
We implement this design in UniRL v0.2.0, validate ten core IR invariants in a source-importing CPU harness, and examine its reward-serving and rollout-engine boundaries through two image-editing case studies on generation--reward overlap and conditioning fidelity.
Across three seeds, SD3.5-Medium diffusion post-training improves held-out automatic preference and compositional scores at the cost of reduced within-prompt diversity (\ETwoDiversityChange{} LPIPS), while in an eight-GPU native-recipe benchmark, UniRL achieves lower per-iteration time than VeRL-Omni (\ReportedUniRLSeconds{}\,s vs.\ \ReportedSDPASeconds{}\,s).
In a recipe-matched 500-rollout Qwen3-4B-Base Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) comparison, UniRL records 8.2\% higher end-to-end output-token throughput than veRL on runs executed concurrently on separate cluster nodes, with UniRL sampling 11.0\% longer responses and a higher 8,192-token truncation rate (0.307 vs.\ 0.203).
On the primary autoregressive endpoint (MATH-500), the prespecified GRPO recipe improves at early checkpoints but degrades by the final 800-rollout checkpoint, ending below the frozen base model across all three seeds; a post-hoc DRPO recipe finishes above the base model across all three seeds under standard grading, though one seed remains above the base only when responses that reach the generation token limit are graded as valid.
In a composed rewrite-to-image workflow, two lineage-defined groupings match an ID-joined reference on recorded rollouts and both improve over the base model in training, while their three-seed quality contrast is inconclusive.
Throughout the evaluation, we distinguish metrics computed directly from exported run artifacts (weight-publication parity, autoregressive and diffusion training, cross-stage grouping, and the rerun autoregressive systems benchmark) from legacy report-derived summaries: isolating the cause of late-stage GRPO degradation, verifying replay-tensor lineage on recorded rollouts, measuring paired IR representation overhead, and establishing a controlled time-to-quality comparison remain open.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Online post-training is a feedback program: it expands inputs into sampled trajectories, evaluates those trajectories, turns feedback into a learning signal, and applies one or more optimizer updates.
At this level of abstraction, autoregressive (AR) text generation, diffusion denoising, and multi-stage multimodal pipelines look nearly identical, yet their underlying data paths and state requirements diverge sharply.
An AR update may need packed token IDs, masks, rollout log probabilities, and per-token critic value estimates; a diffusion update may need a sparse set of latent states, a noise schedule, and transition statistics; a composed workflow must retain which image descended from which rewritten prompt; and an asynchronous run must additionally track which behavior version generated each trajectory layer.

Existing systems have made major progress on scalable LLM post-training and flexible device mapping~\citep{sheng2024hybridflow,hu2024openrlhf,wang2025roll,nemorl2025}.
Fully or partially asynchronous systems further address rollout tail latency and accelerator idle time~\citep{fu2025areal,zhang2026relax,slime2025}.
Post-training is now expanding from single-stage text generation to iterative diffusion denoising and multi-stage multimodal pipelines~\citep{black2023ddpo,liu2025flowgrpo,xue2025dancegrpo,zheng2025diffusionnft,verlomni2026}.
In multi-stage and multimodal workflows, consecutive generation stages differ in modality, replay state, and branching factor while sharing tree ancestry for conditioning and credit assignment, making flat, LLM-specific tensor dictionaries insufficient.
The central systems question is how to preserve shared trajectory structure---lineage, conditioning, credit, and policy provenance---across heterogeneous model states and distributed execution topologies:

\begin{quote}
\emph{Can a single post-training programming model span heterogeneous generative model families and execution topologies while preserving the trajectory structure required for grouping, replay, credit assignment, tensor transport, and behavior-version control?}
\end{quote}

Our answer in UniRL is to introduce a \emph{trajectory intermediate representation (IR)} that captures multi-stage lineage, modality-specific state, and policy provenance across stages with differing batch sizes, and to expose physical execution choices as explicit contracts around that IR.
Rather than treating an individual row or a flat LLM-specific tensor dictionary as the unit of scheduling and sharding, the IR organizes each batch as a forest of prompt-rooted trees whose width expands whenever a stage branches.
Within each tree layer, typed containers separate decoded modality primitives, model-facing conditions, and stage-specific replay segments.
Distributed methods declare how each structured field scatters, concatenates, packs, or remains shared, and dense tensors can be replaced by row-addressable references without changing the trajectory structure.
This representation flows through a small set of logical roles that can be mapped onto colocated or separate \emph{GPU slabs} (contiguous subsets of accelerators allocated to a role).

The paper develops three coupled design ideas:

\begin{itemize}
  \item A \emph{lineage-preserving trajectory IR} in which a rollout request is a typed tree skeleton spanning AR and diffusion stages, each generated layer is conditioned through its ancestry and retains its own replay segment, and training folds reward and credit back along the same tree (Sections~\ref{sec:trajectory-layers}--\ref{sec:fold}). Synthetic fixture checks and an ID-join check on recorded rollouts of a composed prompt-rewrite-to-image run verify the grouping and advantage semantics, while the downstream quality comparison between the two groupings is inconclusive and replay-tensor verification on recorded rollouts remains open.
  \item A \emph{modular interface for stage replay and optimization} that decouples trajectory layers from stage-specific objectives and distributed training backends, reducing the integration complexity of $N_{\mathrm{mod}}$ model bundles and $N_{\mathrm{alg}}$ shared algorithms from $N_{\mathrm{mod}}\times N_{\mathrm{alg}}$ to $N_{\mathrm{mod}}+N_{\mathrm{alg}}$ (Sections~\ref{sec:algorithm-boundary} and~\ref{sec:instantiations}).
  \item A \emph{factorization of semantic roles from physical execution}: rollout engines implement a uniform skeleton-filling transformation ($\sample\!\rightarrow\!\sample$, where \sample{} is the batched trajectory container), while field-aware dispatch, tensor references, placement scopes, weight publication, and memory residency govern execution across colocated, disaggregated, and asynchronous topologies (Section~\ref{sec:execution}), complemented by case studies on reward-serving isolation and overlap and on rollout-engine conditioning fidelity (Sections~\ref{sec:reward} and~\ref{sec:fidelity}). Pre-launch and in-run parity checks across five weight-publication paths, together with native-recipe diffusion, recipe-matched AR, and deployment-topology benchmarks, evaluate these boundaries; paired IR representation overhead remains unmeasured.
\end{itemize}

Asynchronous execution is where the third idea pays off most directly.
Because rollout, reward, and training are logical roles rather than fixed steps of one loop, the orchestration layer---admission, root-complete grouping, version stamping, lag filtering, and weight publication---can be lifted out of individual trainers.
\system's AR and diffusion trainers therefore share one bounded-staleness manager over the same trajectory IR in code (Section~\ref{sec:async}).
Empirically, our exploratory bounded-staleness study exercises only the Qwen3-4B-Base AR workload: single-seed planning runs with evaluation disabled record lower per-rollout time once generation--training overlap is enabled, but the zero-lag disaggregated quality-target pilot degraded between rollouts 150 and 300 and the formal sweep was canceled, so no asynchronous time-to-quality or diffusion-asynchrony result is claimed (Section~\ref{sec:rq4}).

The evidence is organized from structural correctness, through credible learning and a composed workflow, to measured system cost.
The experiment IDs and corresponding artifacts are specified in Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}.

\section{Problem: Preserve Semantics While Changing Execution}
\label{sec:problem}

Let an online post-training iteration consume a batch of initial root inputs $X_0$ (such as text prompts or multimodal contexts), generate a trajectory batch $\mathcal{T}$ under behavior policy $\pi_v$, evaluate rewards $R$ via reward operator $\mathcal{R}$, compute credit $A$ via credit-assignment operator $\mathcal{G}$, and update policy parameters $\theta$ via update operator $U_\theta$:
\begin{equation}
X_0 \xrightarrow{\pi_v} \mathcal{T}
  \xrightarrow{\mathcal{R}} (\mathcal{T},R)
  \xrightarrow{\mathcal{G}} (\mathcal{T},A)
  \xrightarrow{U_\theta} \theta'.
\label{eq:outer-loop}
\end{equation}
The compact notation hides three forms of heterogeneity.

\paragraph{Trajectory heterogeneity.}
The sufficient statistics required for a policy update depend on the generative process.
For AR policies, a replay segment typically stores variable-length packed token sequences, attention and loss masks, rollout log probabilities, and per-token critic value estimates or returns.
For stochastic differential equation (SDE) diffusion or flow policies, a replay segment instead stores latent snapshots at selected denoising steps, a shared noise schedule, the indices of sampled transitions, and rollout transition statistics (such as step log probabilities or transition means).
Multi-stage pipelines and multi-turn agentic workflows further introduce branching lineage and interleaved environment observations.

For example, suppose each root prompt $p$ branches into $k_1$ text rewrites $w_{p,j}$, and each rewrite branches into $k_2$ images $x_{p,j,k}$.
Although the image generator conditions directly on $w_{p,j}$, credit assignment $\mathcal{G}$ may normalize rewards either across the $k_2$ images sharing rewrite $w_{p,j}$ or across all $k_1 k_2$ images descending from root prompt $p$.
Supporting both grouping scopes over a flattened image batch requires explicitly retaining or reconstructing the parent and root identifiers $(p, j)$ for every row.
This branching structure motivates explicit lineage tracking, whereas the diffusion model's denoising state independently motivates typed replay segments; neither mechanism subsumes the other.

\paragraph{Role heterogeneity.}
Generation, reward evaluation, replay, gradient computation, and weight publication differ in runtime, parallel layout, and lifecycle, not only in kernels.
Rollout may run inside the training process or in a dedicated serving engine with its own tensor-parallel (TP) layout, such as SGLang~\citep{zheng2024sglang}, vLLM~\citep{kwon2023vllm}, or vLLM-Omni~\citep{yin2026vllmomni}, while a sharded training backend such as FSDP~\citep{zhao2023fsdp} or VeOmni~\citep{ma2025veomni} owns parameters and optimizer state.
Reward ranges from an in-process scorer to an environment-isolated scorer process or a remote multi-model service.
These roles must agree on when each copy of the policy is resident, offloaded, or published, and publishing from a sharded trainer is itself a collective over the whole training mesh.
Which boundary dominates cost depends on the configuration: in the measured separate-slab deployment, much of the slowdown arises from reward processing (Section~\ref{sec:rq3}).

\paragraph{Topology heterogeneity.}
A small-scale run may colocate and time-share training and rollout on a single GPU allocation; a larger deployment may disaggregate them onto separate GPU slabs; and workloads with variable generation lengths may overlap rollout and training via bounded-staleness asynchronous admission.
Each choice changes memory pressure, transfer paths, synchronization frequency, and allowable policy lag.

These observations produce four requirements (Table~\ref{tab:requirements}).
The first two follow from trajectory heterogeneity and the third from role and topology heterogeneity; the fourth specializes the third to asynchronous execution, where decoupled roles may train on work generated by an older policy.

\paragraph{Non-goals.}
UniRL does not eliminate model-specific code; instead, it isolates architecture-specific forward passes, conditioning encoders, and rollout adapters within a \emph{model bundle}, which interfaces with stage-level algorithms that compute losses over replayed segments.
Nor does UniRL automatically search for optimal device placements or replace the underlying cluster scheduler: the runtime uses a central driver process to orchestrate logical roles and Ray actors~\citep{moritz2018ray} to host distributed workers.

\section{A Trajectory-Centric Programming Model}
\label{sec:trajectory}

\subsection{A trajectory batch is a sequence of tree layers}
\label{sec:trajectory-layers}

The central data type is a trajectory batch
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{T} = [P_0,P_1,\ldots,P_L],
\qquad
P_i = (I_i, X_i, C_i, Z_i, R_i, A_i, V_i, M_i),
\label{eq:sample}
\end{equation}
where, for each depth $i$, $I_i$ stores row identifiers, $X_i$ decoded multimodal primitives, $C_i$ model conditions, $Z_i$ stage-specific replay segments, $R_i$ rewards, $A_i$ advantages or returns, $V_i$ the behavior-policy version $v_b$ stamped by the generating engine (Section~\ref{sec:lifecycle}), and $M_i$ auxiliary metadata.
In code, $\mathcal{T}$ is an instance of \sample{} and each $P_i$ is an instance of the batched class \parttype{}; we refer to $P_i$ as a \emph{tree layer} (or simply \emph{layer}) throughout the paper.
The sequence $[P_0,\ldots,P_L]$ encodes a forest of prompt-rooted trees in which $P_i$ batches all nodes at depth $i$ and lineage edges connect parent rows in $P_{i-1}$ to child rows in $P_i$.
We call the deepest layer $P_L$ the \emph{frontier}; a trajectory batch grows by appending a new layer after the current frontier.
Each generated layer is parameterized by the stage sampling configuration that governs its generation and carries a corresponding replay segment---such as a \texttt{TextSegment} for an AR layer or a \texttt{LatentSegment} for a diffusion layer---whereas input layers carry root prompts or environment observations that condition subsequent generation.

Row identifiers encode tree ancestry directly: root IDs in $I_0$ contain no lineage separator, and each branching step appends a delimiter and child ordinal (e.g., $p_1/0$).
For every non-root layer $P_i$ ($i>0$) and every row identifier $s\in I_i$, the parent identifier must exist in $I_{i-1}$:
\begin{equation}
\forall i>0,\;\forall s\in I_i:\quad \operatorname{parent}(s)\in I_{i-1}.
\label{eq:parent-invariant}
\end{equation}
The \sample{} constructor enforces Equation~\ref{eq:parent-invariant} whenever a batch is built or extended.
Two structural operators grow the tree at the frontier: \texttt{fork} appends a child generation layer with $k$ child rows per frontier row, and \texttt{observe} appends a one-to-one ($k=1$) environment observation layer.
In the reverse direction, \texttt{propagate\_rewards} folds rewards from a scored child layer $P_i$ back onto an unscored parent layer $P_{i-1}$.

Batch-level partitioning and indexing operate on complete root trees rather than individual rows: \texttt{Sample.split()} decomposes a batch into one tree-complete \sample{} per root ID, and slicing or subset selection acts over these root groups.
Manipulating whole root trees preserves the parent-major sibling contiguity established by \texttt{fork}, whereas permuting child rows independently can break the contiguous sibling blocks assumed by group-based reward reduction and advantage estimation.

\paragraph{Well-formed batches.}
Lineage operations require well-formed trajectory batches in which (i) root IDs in $I_0$ are unique and (ii) within every non-root layer $P_i$, sibling rows appear in contiguous parent-major order; when concatenating multiple batches, they must additionally share the same layer depth $L$ and compatible per-layer field metadata.
The programming model maintains these invariants by construction---growing trajectories exclusively through \texttt{fork} and \texttt{observe} and partitioning batches only along root-group boundaries---so reward reduction and group-advantage operators can scan each sibling group as a single contiguous slice.
At runtime, group-advantage computation explicitly rejects non-contiguous sibling layouts, shard concatenation verifies matching layer metadata, and the asynchronous batch assembler enforces homogeneous behavior versions $v_b$ within each admitted batch (validated by our structural regression suite in Appendix~\ref{app:correctness}).

\subsection{Primitives, conditions, and replay segments are separate layer fields}

Within each layer $P_i$, UniRL separates three payload components---decoded primitives $X_i$, model conditions $C_i$, and replay segments $Z_i$---that existing post-training pipelines often conflate.

\paragraph{Primitives ($X_i$).}
Primitives hold decoded or externally supplied modality payloads: text strings, image tensors, video frames, audio waveforms, and external media references.
Variable-resolution images and variable-duration video or audio payloads use packed storage accompanied by per-example shapes or cumulative sequence offsets.
Primitives are consumed by reward evaluators, logging and visualization tools, and multi-turn conditioning, but need not be the low-level tensors replayed during gradient updates.

\paragraph{Conditions ($C_i$).}
Conditions store model-facing encodings, such as prompt embeddings or reference image latents.
Separating $C_i$ from $X_i$ lets rollout engines and training stages exchange precomputed conditioning tensors through a typed contract without prescribing a single tokenizer, text encoder, or multimodal fusion architecture.

\paragraph{Replay segments ($Z_i$).}
Replay segments store the stage-specific sufficient statistics required to recompute policy likelihoods or transitions during training.
For autoregressive stages, \texttt{TextSegment} stores packed token IDs, attention masks, and rollout log probabilities.
For diffusion and flow stages, \texttt{LatentSegment} stores latent snapshots at a subset of denoising steps together with their step-index map, a shared $\sigma$-schedule, and optional SDE transition log probabilities or means.
Recording a sparse step-index map allows diffusion objectives to replay only selected denoising transitions rather than materializing the full denoising trajectory during backpropagation.

This separation decouples the lifetime of decoded media from the lifetime of replay state: once reward evaluation and logging complete, the trainer can discard large decoded primitives $X_i$ while retaining conditions $C_i$, replay segments $Z_i$, rewards $R_i$, and advantages $A_i$ for the training update.

\subsection{A field algebra defines structural batching}

Every field in a structured batch declares a composition kind: \emph{concatenated} along the batch dimension, \emph{packed variable-length}, \emph{shared} across rows (the default for unannotated fields), or \emph{scalar-reduced} under concatenation via maximum, minimum, sum, or mean (used in the current runtime to reduce per-shard timing metadata by maximum).
Structural operations---concatenation, root-group selection, slicing, and repetition---traverse nested objects recursively, transforming concatenated and packed fields while passing shared fields through unchanged; device transfers apply to all underlying tensors regardless of kind.

The same field algebra governs distributed argument scattering and return-value reconstruction across role workers (Section~\ref{sec:execution}).
When a method is invoked under data-parallel (DP) scatter, an input \sample{} is partitioned into complete root groups across the DP shards (requiring the root count to be divisible by the DP degree), while auxiliary arguments whose leading dimension does not match the split count are replicated to each shard.
Upon completion, the results returned by the primary rank of each DP shard are merged layer-by-layer according to each field's declared kind; for broadcast lifecycle methods, the runtime instead returns the per-worker result list so callers expecting identical state can verify cross-worker agreement explicitly.

Declaring batching semantics at the field level eliminates hand-written tensor slicing and padding inside individual roles, while relying on the well-formed batch invariants of Section~\ref{sec:trajectory-layers} to guarantee valid shard merges.
Because recursive tree traversal and tensor-reference bookkeeping (Section~\ref{sec:execution}) introduce non-trivial CPU overhead, we benchmark their cost directly in Section~\ref{sec:rq3} rather than assuming the abstraction is free.

\subsection{Rollout unfolds a declared trajectory tree}
\label{sec:unfold}

A rollout request is a trajectory batch that declares the skeleton of the tree to be generated.
Starting from an initial root batch $[P_0]$, the caller appends one target generation layer per stage via \texttt{fork}; prior to execution by a rollout engine, we refer to each unfilled generation layer as a \emph{shell}.
Each shell pre-allocates its row count ($k$ children per parent row), populates child identifiers $I_i$ that encode their parent IDs in $I_{i-1}$, and attaches a stage-specific sampling-parameter object---such as $s^{\mathrm{AR}}$ or $s^{\mathrm{diff}}$---that specifies the generative process to run.
For example, the prompt-enhancement and unified-model trainers construct the two-stage skeleton
\begin{equation}
\sample([P_0]).\texttt{fork}(k_1,s^{\mathrm{AR}}).\texttt{fork}(k_2,s^{\mathrm{diff}})
= [P_0,\;P_1^{\mathrm{AR}},\;P_2^{\mathrm{diff}}],
\label{eq:skeleton}
\end{equation}
where $s^{\mathrm{AR}}$ and $s^{\mathrm{diff}}$ denote the autoregressive and diffusion sampling parameters.
For $B$ root prompts, the $B k_1$ text rows in $P_1^{\mathrm{AR}}$ and $B k_1 k_2$ image rows in $P_2^{\mathrm{diff}}$ have fixed lineage before any token or latent is sampled.
Every rollout engine implements a skeleton-filling transformation
\begin{equation}
F_{\theta,e}: \mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{skeleton}} \longrightarrow \mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{filled}},
\label{eq:engine}
\end{equation}
parameterized by policy weights $\theta$ and generation runtime $e$ (either a train-side model pipeline or an external serving engine).
The operator $F_{\theta,e}$ preserves the skeleton's layer structure and row identifiers $I_i$, populates each target shell with decoded primitives $X_i$, optional conditions $C_i$, and a replay segment $Z_i$, and stamps each filled layer with the behavior version $V_i = v_b$ of the engine that generated it.
Alongside $F_{\theta,e}$, the base engine interface exposes residency-lifecycle and weight-publication methods (Section~\ref{sec:lifecycle}) without constraining the runtime's internal serving scheduler or kernel API.

\paragraph{One trajectory spans autoregressive and diffusion stages.}
In a multi-stage workflow, a single trajectory batch captures all generation stages in sequence.
When populating shell $P_i$, the rollout engine queries \texttt{conditioning\_at} to walk each row's ancestor chain $P_0,\ldots,P_{i-1}$ and collect the modality-tagged primitives along that path.
In the text-to-text-and-image modes of HunyuanImage-3.0 and BAGEL, for instance, the first stage fills the AR shell $P_1^{\mathrm{AR}}$ from the root prompt in $P_0$, and the diffusion stage then reads both the root prompt and the generated reasoning text directly through each image row's ancestry in $P_2^{\mathrm{diff}}$, eliminating external side tables to join images with rewritten prompts.
Because each stage writes its replay state into its own layer---a \texttt{TextSegment} in $P_1^{\mathrm{AR}}$ and a \texttt{LatentSegment} in $P_2^{\mathrm{diff}}$---while sharing the same tree lineage $I_0, I_1, I_2$, the filled \sample{} retains both the ancestry required for cross-stage credit assignment and the stage-specific sufficient statistics required for policy replay (Section~\ref{sec:fold}).
An AR stage and a diffusion stage are thus represented as consecutive typed layers of one prompt-rooted tree rather than separate batches joined after generation.
Using this single representation, our trainers express single-stage AR, single-stage diffusion, two-stage AR-to-diffusion, and multi-turn tool-use trajectories simply by varying the declared skeleton.

\paragraph{Engine forms.}
The runtime provides two direct implementations of Equation~\ref{eq:engine} and two compositional coordinators built over them.
First, a train-side adapter invokes the model's forward generation pipeline directly in evaluation mode to fill the shells of a request in process.
Second, dedicated serving adapters for SGLang, SGLang-Diffusion, vLLM, vLLM-Omni, and FastVideo translate ancestor primitives and stage sampling parameters into native serving requests and pack the outputs back into typed layer fields.
At the compositional level, a multi-stage composed engine implements Equation~\ref{eq:engine} over a fixed multi-shell skeleton such as Equation~\ref{eq:skeleton} by invoking child engines sequentially across stages, whereas a multi-turn agentic harness composes Equation~\ref{eq:engine} dynamically across interaction turns---alternating \texttt{fork}, single-turn engine execution, and \texttt{observe} to return a trajectory with additional layers.
Equation~\ref{eq:engine} thus standardizes the trajectory data boundary rather than imposing a single generation kernel.

\subsection{Training folds the tree into per-stage updates}
\label{sec:fold}

After rollout completes, the post-rollout pass folds feedback from the frontier back toward the root before executing per-stage parameter updates.
First, the reward role scores the frontier layer---such as the image layer $P_2^{\mathrm{diff}}$ in Equation~\ref{eq:skeleton}.
Next, \texttt{propagate\_rewards} reduces rewards from each scored child layer $P_i$ onto its unscored parent layer $P_{i-1}$ via mean, maximum, or sum, propagating downstream image feedback to the upstream AR rewrite layer $P_1^{\mathrm{AR}}$.
For group-relative objectives, \texttt{compute\_advantages} then assigns credit on each trainable layer by grouping rows according to their ancestor ID at a specified depth---by default the immediate parent in $P_{i-1}$, or the root prompt in $P_0$---and normalizing rewards within each group.
On the image layer of Equation~\ref{eq:skeleton}, these two depths correspond to comparing images generated from the same rewrite versus comparing all images generated from the same root prompt; switching between them requires only a lineage depth parameter rather than an external table join (Section~\ref{sec:rq3}).
Both reward reduction and group normalization rely on the sibling-contiguity invariant of Section~\ref{sec:trajectory-layers} to process each sibling group as a contiguous slice.
Group-normalized advantages are computed and attached on the driver before data-parallel scattering so that training shards receive precomputed credit without reconstructing sibling groups (whereas token-level GAE is computed per sequence on the training workers; Section~\ref{sec:instantiations}).

During the update phase, the trainer routes each trainable layer $P_i$---matched by its stage sampling-parameter type---to a corresponding \emph{training track} that pairs a stage algorithm with a \texttt{TrainStack}, while skipping frozen layers.
In a decoupled two-stage workflow such as prompt enhancement, the AR rewrite model and the diffusion generator maintain separate training stacks and optimizer steps; freezing the rewrite stage simply disables the AR track so only the diffusion layer is replayed and updated.
When both stages share a single backbone, as in HunyuanImage-3.0, a unified training stack replays both the AR and diffusion layers and back-propagates their combined losses through a single shared optimizer step.

\subsection{Component interfaces for stage replay and optimization}
\label{sec:algorithm-boundary}

Each training track separates stage optimization into three interacting abstractions: a trajectory-layer projection, a stage algorithm, and a training stack.

\paragraph{Trajectory-layer projection.}
For a trainable stage at depth $i$, layer $P_i$ supplies the core tuple $(C_i, Z_i, A_i)$ comprising model-facing conditions $C_i$, the stage replay segment $Z_i$ (\texttt{TextSegment} or \texttt{LatentSegment}), and advantages $A_i$.

\paragraph{Stage algorithm.}
The stage algorithm encapsulates the stage-specific loss evaluation and backward pass over a single shard:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{stage}}:\; (C_i, Z_i, A_i;\,\theta) \longmapsto (\mathcal{L}(\theta),\, \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}(\theta)).
\label{eq:algorithm}
\end{equation}
The core objective signature consumes only $(C_i, Z_i, A_i)$ and the active model parameters $\theta$.
When an objective requires auxiliary per-row state---such as metadata in $M_i$ or token-level critic value estimates stored in the segment---an optional per-shard preparation hook reads the enclosing \parttype{} $P_i$ before micro-batching; algorithms that require a frozen reference policy or exponential moving average (EMA) teacher hold an explicit backend model handle.

\paragraph{Train stack.}
The \texttt{TrainStack} partitions shards into micro-batches, optionally caches an old-policy replay anchor under the training micro-batch layout prior to multi-epoch updates, orchestrates gradient accumulation and clipping, reduces global loss metrics across ranks, and steps the optimizer.

Under this division of responsibilities, AR objectives replay token log probabilities from \texttt{TextSegment}s and diffusion objectives replay selected denoising transitions from \texttt{LatentSegment}s, comparing the replayed likelihoods or transition statistics against those recorded during rollout, cached from a pre-update replay pass, or evaluated under a reference model.
Supervised fine-tuning and teacher-distillation objectives ignore $A_i$, whereas the forward-process DiffusionNFT objective consumes group advantages $A_i$ through the same $(C_i, Z_i, A_i)$ interface.

Because model bundles and stage algorithms interact solely through typed layer fields, they compose without pairwise glue code.
Each model family is integrated once as a bundle whose rollout adapters and trainable stages populate and replay typed segments ($C_i, Z_i$), and each algorithm is implemented once as an objective over those replayed segments.
Any shared algorithm can therefore pair with any model stage that produces and replays the required segment type---AR objectives with \texttt{TextSegment} stages and diffusion objectives with \texttt{LatentSegment} stages---reducing the integration complexity of $N_{\mathrm{mod}}$ models and $N_{\mathrm{alg}}$ shared algorithms from $N_{\mathrm{mod}}\times N_{\mathrm{alg}}$ to $N_{\mathrm{mod}}+N_{\mathrm{alg}}$.
When a model requires a custom loss, it introduces a stage-algorithm subclass without modifying existing model bundles or shared objectives; conversely, variants such as DanceGRPO and MixGRPO that retain the \texttt{FlowGRPO} loss~\citep{liu2025flowgrpo} and alter only the SDE transition kernel or step schedule are expressed as configuration recipes rather than new algorithm classes.

We do not claim algorithmic novelty in the objectives themselves: our Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) implementation follows the group-relative formulation of~\citet{shao2024deepseekmath}, our Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) implementation follows the clipped surrogate objective of~\citet{schulman2017ppo}, and our diffusion objectives directly implement prior diffusion-RL estimators~\citep{black2023ddpo,liu2025flowgrpo,xue2025dancegrpo,zheng2025diffusionnft}.
Instead, the systems question is whether a single trajectory IR and execution substrate can support all of these objectives while faithfully preserving their distinct sufficient statistics.

\subsection{Logical roles, device slabs, and worker slots}

A \texttt{DevicePool} creates one Ray worker per GPU initially and reserves one strict-packed placement group per node.
Each worker occupies a slot on its GPU: slot~0 exists from startup, further slots are created on demand up to a per-GPU limit, and every slot reserves an equal fraction of that GPU.
A top-level placement scope claims a fraction of the whole device pool from the still-unclaimed devices; a nested scope takes a fraction of its parent's devices.
Roles instantiated within a scope either share the scope's existing worker slot on each device---enabling direct in-process sibling access and memory time-sharing---or allocate isolated slots on those same devices, which communicate through the per-GPU store transport.
Disjoint top-level scopes form separate training, rollout, or reward slabs.

Each role is defined as a Python class derived from \texttt{Remote}.
Instantiating a role inside a placement scope returns a driver-side handle that constructs one role replica on each selected worker and derives data-, tensor-, pipeline-, sequence-, and expert-parallel rank metadata from the role configuration.
Method decorators declare which ranks execute and how the handle dispatches arguments and reassembles return values (broadcast to all target ranks, positional shard scatter, or root-group DP scatter using the field algebra of Section~\ref{sec:trajectory}).
The trainer is thus a single-controller program on the driver, while each dispatched method may run multi-controller distributed training or serving across the role's workers.
This control split resembles HybridFlow's separation of orchestration from distributed computation~\citep{sheng2024hybridflow}; \system differs by routing a single lineage-preserving, multimodal trajectory IR across heterogeneous generative stages.

\subsection{Tensor references decouple structure from movement}

In the single-controller design, every role method is a remote call issued by the driver, and its result returns to the driver before being dispatched to the next role.
If trajectories carried dense tensors across these hops, latent snapshots, token log probabilities, and decoded media would pass through the driver on every call, and the driver would have to materialize them merely to split, select, or scatter complete root groups.
UniRL therefore routes control through the driver but keeps tensor data on the workers.
When a role method returns, each participating worker stores its dense tensor leaves in a local transport store and \emph{dehydrates} each tensor into a \texttt{TensorRef}: an ordered list of half-open row spans over opaque handles, together with shape, dtype, and device metadata.
The driver receives only this dehydrated trajectory, so root-group selection and contiguous slicing construct new span views without moving tensor payloads.
When a subsequent call targets a different role or shard layout, cross-worker transports localize each reference for its destination worker, leaving already-resident spans in place and transferring remote spans worker-to-worker.
Each destination worker then \emph{hydrates} all referenced spans in a single batch---zero-padding spans whose trailing dimensions differ to a common width---and writes the dense tensors back into the nested object in place before invoking the method.
Driver-side control logic materializes only small scalar or vector fields, such as rewards, on demand.

The transport interface exposes four operations: store tensors, resolve handles, identify references, and (for cross-worker backends) localize references for destination workers.
UniRL provides (i) an in-process colocated per-worker store, (ii) a per-GPU store using CUDA inter-process communication (IPC) and NVIDIA Collective Communications Library (NCCL) transfers, and (iii) a TransferQueue path backed by either built-in in-memory storage (\texttt{SimpleStorage}) or Mooncake-compatible distributed storage~\citep{qin2024mooncake}; we use Mooncake's storage interface purely as a transport backend rather than a claimed contribution.

The driver can therefore reorganize trajectory lineage and metadata without gathering dense GPU payloads onto the controller.
Whether this indirection yields net memory and transfer savings relative to flat ID-joined rows is an empirical question under RQ3 (Section~\ref{sec:rq3}; the paired E5-R microbenchmark remains open) rather than an a priori assumption.

\subsection{Residency lifecycles and weight publication}
\label{sec:lifecycle}

Training and generation often hold different copies or formats of a policy.
We distinguish two version counters: the \emph{optimizer version} $v_t$, which counts committed optimizer steps on the training stack, and the \emph{behavior version} $v_b$, which the rollout engine stamps onto each generated frontier layer.
In every deployment, a due weight publication completes before the generation it serves; how publication interleaves with memory residency depends on whether training and rollout share devices.

\paragraph{Colocated slabs.}
When training and rollout time-share a single GPU slab, the rollout engine is put to sleep---releasing its weight and KV-cache memory---between generations and woken before the next rollout, while training state may be offloaded to host memory while the engine is awake.
All colocated schedules preserve two invariants (Appendix~\ref{app:residency}): any due weight publication completes before generation begins, and the engine is put back to sleep before offloaded training state is restored for the training update.
When no offload is needed during wake-up, the default schedule (used by the AR, agentic, and prompt-enhancement trainers) wakes the engine, publishes weights if due, offloads training state if enabled, and runs rollout, after which it puts the engine to sleep and restores the training state.
When peak GPU memory cannot hold both the full training state and an awake engine, the AR and unified-model trainers use one of three adaptations (Table~\ref{tab:residency-orders}): (i) if no publication is due, offload training state before waking the engine; (ii) if the engine can allocate weight memory separately from KV cache, wake weight memory first, publish weights, offload training state (or offload optimizer state before waking weights and model shards after publishing for vLLM's native weight-transfer engine), and then allocate the KV cache; or (iii) when an AR engine is pinned to a single GPU of the slab or a unified-model trainer drives separate AR and diffusion engines, extract due weights first, offload training state, wake the engine(s), and publish the extracted weights.
The diffusion trainers configure residency per role (training, rollout, reward): before waking the engine, they extract the adapter if training will be offloaded and offload every non-resident role; they then wake the engine, publish weights if due, put the engine to sleep after generation unless the rollout role is marked resident, and restore the offloaded training stack before its replay and optimizer update.
A train-side rollout adapter reads the live training model directly, so training state is never offloaded for it.

\paragraph{Separate slabs.}
When training and rollout occupy disjoint GPU slabs, the two roles do not contend for device memory:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item at startup, establish a one-time communication handshake between the training shards and rollout workers;
  \item at each publication boundary, transfer updated parameters across slabs (subject to the quiescence discipline of Section~\ref{sec:async} for asynchronous trainers);
  \item generate trajectories and stamp the frontier layer with the engine's current behavior version $v_b$.
\end{enumerate}
Rollout never offloads training state to free space for the engine.
On its separate slabs, the synchronous diffusion trainer still puts the rollout engine to sleep between rollouts unless the rollout role is configured as resident, and offloads training state after generation only when a non-resident training role time-shares the training slab with a reward model.
The asynchronous trainers keep the rollout engine awake throughout training, and the asynchronous diffusion trainer additionally requires a resident reward role.

Weight-publication handlers (\codepath{unirl/distributed/weight\_sync/}) support both in-process and cross-slab Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) adapter transfer as well as full-parameter publication via serialized tensor payloads (\texttt{TensorWeightSync}), CUDA IPC (with handles exchanged over ZeroMQ or through vLLM's native weight-transfer engine), SGLang checkpoint-engine IPC, NCCL broadcast, and filesystem checkpoint handoff (\texttt{CheckpointWeightSync}).
For multi-stage models, a composed rollout engine can also route per-track weight updates to the targeted child engine.

Version stamping depends on the trainer path.
The asynchronous trainers (via the rollout manager; Section~\ref{sec:async}) and the synchronous agentic trainer set $v_b = v_t$ upon each weight publication, so $v_b$ records exact optimizer-version provenance.
The remaining synchronous trainers (AR, diffusion, prompt-enhancement, and unified-model) leave $v_b = 0$ across rollouts, except when configured with vLLM's native weight-transfer engine, which increments $v_b$ by one on each received publication.

The source rejects combinations whose lifecycle has not been validated, such as a train-side rollout on a physically separate slab or selected EMA/offload combinations.
This fail-closed policy is important because a partially awakened engine or silently stale adapter can produce plausible but invalid training data.
Section~\ref{sec:evaluation} therefore includes replay-consistency and failure-injection checks alongside throughput.

\subsection{Synchronous execution}

The synchronous AR trainer performs rollout, reward, credit assignment, replay/update, optional evaluation, and checkpointing in order.
The synchronous diffusion trainer can accumulate multiple rollout batches into a single optimizer window; spatially, it can colocate the reward role on the training slab, isolate it on a third GPU slab, or query an external reward service, and temporally, it can pipeline micro-batched reward scoring inside the rollout window to overlap generation with evaluation (Section~\ref{sec:reward}).
Across modalities, deployment topology is selected through the same placement-scope primitives.
The synchronous AR trainer allocates a single colocated slab (optionally pinning the rollout engine to one GPU), whereas the synchronous diffusion trainer supports either a single colocated slab or separate training and rollout slabs.
Disaggregated synchronous AR execution (\texttt{D0} in Section~\ref{sec:rq4}) is realized by running the asynchronous AR trainer (Section~\ref{sec:async}) with one prompt batch in flight, weight publication after every batch, and zero allowed policy lag ($\ell=0$), so that rollout and training strictly alternate across separate slabs.
In all cases, changing topology alters device ownership, residency scheduling, and weight publication without altering the trajectory IR boundary: the same \sample{} object flows from rollout through reward and credit assignment to the training stack.
This invariance enables controlled deployment comparisons that hold the model, prompt stream, group size, reward function, generation and replay step or token budgets, and optimizer updates per rollout fixed while varying the slab layout, alongside the resulting changes in per-role DP width, residency handoff, and publication path (Section~\ref{sec:rq3}).

\subsection{Bounded-staleness execution}
\label{sec:async}

Asynchrony changes correctness conditions, not just control flow.
UniRL's asynchronous AR and diffusion trainers run on separate training and rollout slabs and share one manager.
The driver submits whole prompt batches to the rollout manager only while they fit within the in-flight batch capacity, the freshness horizon implied by the staleness allowance, and the batch count remaining before the next evaluation, checkpoint, or end-of-training boundary.
A background dispatcher then feeds prompts to each rollout engine replica up to its configured request-concurrency limit.
As sibling completions return, the manager assembles them by root ID; only complete root-prompt trees enter the ready buffer, and every generated layer must carry a behavior version $v_b$.

Let $v_t$ denote the current optimizer version (counting committed optimizer steps) and $u$ the configured number of optimizer updates per consumed rollout batch.
The manager bounds policy lag in optimizer-update units:
\begin{equation}
\ell = v_t-v_b \leq u b,
\end{equation}
where $b$ is the staleness allowance in rollout-batch units (configurable for AR, and fixed to the publication interval minus one for diffusion).
The lag filter assigns each root-complete tree the behavior version of its oldest generated layer and discards whole root groups with $\ell > u b$.
Because a skipped non-finite gradient step does not advance $v_t$, an admitted group can span more than $b$ elapsed rollout batches while still satisfying $\ell \leq u b$.
When a weight publication is due at optimizer version $v_t$, the trainer and manager execute a quiescence barrier: the manager pauses new admission, finishes any root groups already in progress (or the prefix of groups needed to complete an unmixed single-version training batch), and discards any partially generated root groups that would otherwise straddle the version change while retaining queued root prompts that have not yet started generation (\emph{pristine carry}).
The trainer then publishes weights from the training stack to the rollout engine, the manager updates the engine's behavior version to $v_t$, and those unstarted root prompts are resubmitted under the new version.
Evaluation and checkpointing require the manager's in-flight and ready buffers to be completely drained.

This bounded-staleness discipline differs from fully asynchronous optimization: weight publication remains a synchronous barrier, and no training batch may mix behavior versions.
The asynchronous diffusion trainer admits at most one prompt batch in flight and requires each training batch to come from a single generation request, so the next rollout overlaps the training slab's replay and optimizer update phase; the asynchronous AR trainer can keep multiple batches in flight and dispatches replacement rollout requests to the manager before scoring rewards on the newly popped batch.
Consequently, an empirical evaluation must report realized lag distributions, discarded work, and publication barrier time rather than relying on a nominal ``async'' label.

\section{Instantiations of the Common Contracts}
\label{sec:instantiations}

The following instantiations show which information crosses the common trajectory boundary and which computations remain stage-specialized.
They illustrate the contracts at the design level rather than serving as an exhaustive inventory of supported models.
Among these instantiations, the case studies (Sections~\ref{sec:reward} and~\ref{sec:fidelity}) and main evaluation (Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}) exercise single-stage AR post-training with group-relative objectives (GRPO in E0, E1, E3-AR, and E6; DRPO in E1), single-stage diffusion and flow post-training with Flow-GRPO (E0, E2, E3, E5-T, and the BAGEL, HunyuanImage-3.0, and MiniMax-H3 runs), and the cross-stage prompt-enhancement workflow with a frozen AR rewriter and trained diffusion stage (E4).
By contrast, critic-based token-level GAE, the forward-process DiffusionNFT objective, joint training of the upstream rewrite stage, and multi-turn tool-interacting agentic workflows are supported with stock recipes in UniRL but are not benchmarked in Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}.

\subsection{Autoregressive reasoning}

An input layer $P_0$ carries root prompt text and metadata, and forking by the group size $k$ appends a generated shell $P_1^{\mathrm{AR}}$ with parent-contiguous sibling IDs.
An AR rollout engine fills each row's decoded text and packed \texttt{TextSegment}, after which the reward role attaches scalar or multi-component rewards.
Group-normalized advantages are computed on the driver before the batch is scattered to training workers; when critic-based token-level GAE is used instead, advantages and returns are computed independently per generated sequence on the training workers after the train stack freezes the critic anchor.
On each training shard, the train stack plans token-budgeted micro-batches, replays the AR stage on each \texttt{TextSegment}, and invokes the stage algorithm to evaluate the policy loss and backward pass.
Before dispatching the training layer across DP shards, the trainer may optionally apply token-length shard balancing, which permutes individual generated rows after driver-side group credit (and before per-sequence GAE, which is invariant to row order).
This permutation preserves each row's identifier and precomputed advantage but intentionally breaks sibling contiguity, so no group-level reduction or normalization may follow it; in our evaluation, both the E1 GRPO recipe and E3-AR enable shard balancing, whereas the E1 DRPO recipe disables it (Section~\ref{sec:rq1}).

\subsection{Diffusion and flow generation}

To construct a diffusion request, the trainer forks the root prompt layer and records the stage sampling parameters, selected SDE transition indices, and initial-noise specification on the generated shell; the rollout engine then pins the $\sigma$-schedule onto the shell before denoising.
Generation populates decoded media together with a \texttt{LatentSegment} whose stored denoising states and schedule metadata supply the sufficient statistics for the chosen stage algorithm.
Reverse-process objectives such as Flow-GRPO replay the recorded SDE transitions and compare current transition log probabilities against a frozen rollout or pre-update replay anchor~\citep{liu2025flowgrpo}.
Forward-process objectives such as DiffusionNFT instead operate on terminal clean latents rather than reverse-transition likelihood ratios~\citep{zheng2025diffusionnft}.
Both families share the same train stack for micro-batching, gradient accumulation, and optimizer state, while the stage algorithm encapsulates their incompatible loss estimators.

\subsection{Cross-stage and environment-interacting trajectories}

In a prompt-enhancement workflow, the AR stage expands each root prompt into $k_1$ text rewrites and the diffusion stage expands each rewrite into $k_2$ images (Equation~\ref{eq:skeleton}).
The reward role scores the leaf image layer $P_2^{\mathrm{diff}}$; when the rewrite stage is trained, \texttt{propagate\_rewards} reduces each sibling block onto its parent rewrite in $P_1^{\mathrm{AR}}$.
In our E4 study (Section~\ref{sec:rq3}), the rewrite stage is held frozen and lineage is used to select the normalization scope for diffusion group advantages---grouping either the $k_2$ images under each rewrite or all $k_1 k_2$ images descended from the same root prompt.
Preserving this ancestry across rollout scheduling and DP sharding avoids reconstructing prompt--rewrite--image correspondence via out-of-band joins; E4 verifies that both grouping scopes match an explicit ID-joined reference on synthetic fixtures and on recorded rollouts from the composed run, and then compares the two scopes across three-seed training runs (where both improve over the base model and their quality difference is inconclusive).

In a multi-turn tool-interacting workflow, each rollout episode forms a single-branch chain in which the policy emits an action-like text layer, the environment appends an observation layer via \texttt{observe}, and interaction repeats until termination or suspension.
Because episodes sampled from the same prompt can terminate after different numbers of turns (yielding different layer depths $L$), the agentic trainer maintains one branch-one \sample{} per episode and forms a prompt group from repeated copies of the root prompt rather than siblings inside a single \sample{}.
It then computes group-normalized advantages across the episodes in each group and flattens their generated turns for training, with every turn inheriting its episode's advantage; here, trajectory lineage serves to condition each turn on its full interaction history rather than to define intra-\sample{} sibling groups.

Before turning to the main E0--E6 evaluation program in Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}, Sections~\ref{sec:reward} and~\ref{sec:fidelity} present two targeted case studies that examine how the reward-serving and rollout-engine contracts behave on instruction-guided image editing.

\section{Evaluation: Reported Results and Remaining Evidence}
\label{sec:evaluation}

We organize the main evaluation around four research questions (Table~\ref{tab:experiment-map}).
Throughout the synchronous training and systems benchmarks below, one outer-loop iteration collects one rollout batch and applies the configured optimizer updates; we therefore refer to training progress in \emph{rollouts} (and to per-rollout wall-clock duration in systems benchmarks as \emph{iteration} or \emph{step} time).
The prespecified artifact contract requires each run to archive its resolved configuration, source commit and patch state, model and dataset revisions, environment manifest, structured metrics, and evaluation outputs (Appendix~\ref{app:artifacts}); the paragraph below notes where current bundles remain incomplete.
The prespecified protocol requires primary comparisons to run one framework at a time on the same reserved hardware; E3 follows this single-node protocol, whereas the two E3-AR runs executed concurrently on separate two-node H20 allocations in the same cluster (Section~\ref{sec:rq2}).\footnote{Throughout the paper, we follow each baseline project's official capitalization: \emph{veRL} for the language-model post-training framework~\citep{sheng2024hybridflow} and \emph{VeRL-Omni} for the multimodal and diffusion extension~\citep{verlomni2026}.}

\subsection{RQ1: Does UniRL learn on representative workloads?}
\label{sec:rq1}

The first question is scientific rather than purely operational.
A complete training-validity claim requires improvement over the frozen base together with satisfactory collapse diagnostics.
E2 reports an improvement in automatic scores but triggers the diversity guard, so its result is a qualified quality--diversity trade-off.
E1's prespecified GRPO recipe fails the first condition at its registered endpoint; a Divergence-Regularized Policy Optimization (DRPO)~\citep{yao2026rethinking} recipe added later meets the first condition in all three seeds, but one seed fails the second because most of its final-checkpoint answers run to the generation token limit.
Reproducing another implementation additionally requires a full-budget, mathematically aligned reference with common quality endpoints; a systems speed run alone cannot supply that evidence.
The VeRL-Omni reference training run (E2-R) has been trained across three seeds but has not been evaluated head-to-head on held-out prompts, while the adapted AR systems launcher (E3-AR) has not yet been evaluated on the aligned external quality benchmark.

\paragraph{AR workload.}
E1 trains Qwen3-4B-Base~\citep{yang2025qwen3} with full-parameter GRPO on DAPO-Math-17k~\citep{yu2025dapo}.
The dataset name does not imply use of the DAPO optimizer; we fix the population-standard-deviation group estimator implemented in the standard GRPO recipe.
Each rollout collects 64 prompts with eight samples per prompt (512 trajectories) via SGLang (TP=1) with thinking mode enabled, temperature $1.0$, top-$p$ $1.0$, top-$k$ disabled, and a maximum of 8,192 new tokens.
The learner uses group-standard-deviation normalization, symmetric clipping at 0.2, per-sequence token-mean loss (\texttt{seq-mean-token-mean}), four optimizer updates per rollout under a 10,240-token per-GPU micro-batch budget, AdamW (learning rate $10^{-6}$, weight decay $0.01$), and full-weight publication every rollout.
We prespecify 800 rollouts on 32 GPUs, internal AIME validation every ten rollouts, full checkpoints every 200 rollouts, and seed IDs 11, 22, and 33 intended to govern both data order and the SGLang server random number generator (RNG).
These seed IDs and the checkpoint cadence are passed as run-time overrides because the stock recipe defaults to data-source seed 42; because per-request SGLang sampling seeds require deterministic inference mode, the protocol specified seeding the server RNG via engine arguments (see the deviation noted below).
The prespecified external evaluation scores the frozen base model and checkpoints at rollouts 200, 400, 600, and 800 on MATH-500 (500 problems, avg@4) and AIME 2024/2025 (30 problems each, avg@16) with the \texttt{math\_verify} grader under fixed decoding parameters (including a 16,384-token generation limit, twice the training cap, at a lower temperature).
The UniRL run and the reference baseline must align prompts, chat template, verifier, maximum generation length, group size, effective batch, clipping, KL treatment, optimizer updates, and evaluation decoding.
The public veRL Qwen3 FSDP example serves only as a starting scaffold because its stock model variant, dataset, group size, response budget, and KL/objective settings differ.
E3-AR uses a pinned adapted launcher whose resolved recipes are archived and checked field by field against fifteen cross-framework effective-work identities in \codepath{artifacts/reported\_results/2026-10-11-qwen3-4b-grpo/recipe\_parity\_audit.json}; two residual estimator-level differences that configuration cannot remove are quantified there, and the bundle documentation lists the recipe items outside those fifteen identities. Absent frozen-base and checkpoint evaluations under the common external protocol, E3-AR does not by itself establish quality reproduction.
The final 800-rollout checkpoint is primary. Internal evaluation uses AIME 2024/2025, so an AIME-selected best checkpoint is reported only as validation-selected and cannot establish an untouched AIME test result.
MATH-500 is the primary external AR endpoint, with a training/evaluation overlap audit required before interpretation; the fixed-budget AIME curves are secondary diagnostics.
The protocol calls for every seed, final-checkpoint curves, and 95\% confidence intervals; avg@$k$ denotes mean accuracy across $k$ sampled answers, not pass@$k$.

\paragraph{E1 result: GRPO gains early and ends below the base model.}
All three prespecified GRPO seeds completed 800 rollouts, and every surviving checkpoint was scored on the external benchmarks (Table~\ref{tab:rq1-ar}, Figure~\ref{fig:e1-endpoint}).
At rollout 200 the two seeds with a saved checkpoint score \EOneMathGRPOEarlyTwentyTwo{} and \EOneMathGRPOEarlyThirtyThree{} on MATH-500, against \EOneMathBase{} for the frozen base.
Every later checkpoint is lower than the one before it in every seed, and the final checkpoints score \EOneMathGRPOEndEleven{}, \EOneMathGRPOEndTwentyTwo{}, and \EOneMathGRPOEndThirtyThree{}.
No seed improves on the base at the registered endpoint, so E1 does not show the improvement over the frozen base that a training-validity claim requires.
The three final deficits relative to the base model range from \EOneMathGRPODeficitMin{} to \EOneMathGRPODeficitMax{} (mean \EOneMathGRPODeficitMean{}), with a seed-level 95\% confidence interval of [\EOneMathGRPOChangeLow{}, \EOneMathGRPOChangeHigh{}] for the net change from base; all three seeds agree on the negative direction, though with two degrees of freedom the interval is too wide to bound the magnitude tightly.
Mean training reward declines over the same span in every seed---from \EOneRewardEarlyEleven{}, \EOneRewardEarlyTwentyTwo{}, and \EOneRewardEarlyThirtyThree{} over rollouts 101--200 to \EOneRewardLateEleven{}, \EOneRewardLateTwentyTwo{}, and \EOneRewardLateThirtyThree{} over rollouts 701--800---ruling out classic overfitting where training reward rises while held-out accuracy falls.
Responses from the final checkpoints also fail to terminate more frequently: \EOneLimitGRPOEleven{}, \EOneLimitGRPOTwentyTwo{}, and \EOneLimitGRPOThirtyThree{} of their MATH-500 completions reach the 16,384-token generation limit, compared with \EOneLimitBase{} for the frozen base, and at most \EOneLimitCorrectGRPOMax{} of those truncated responses are graded correct.
(We classify a response as reaching the limit when its stored text, once re-tokenized, spans at least 97\% of the 16,384-token cap.)
Non-termination accounts for part, but not all, of the accuracy decline.
Between rollouts 200 and 800, seed 22's share of responses at the limit rises from \EOneLimitGRPOTwentyTwoEarly{} to \EOneLimitGRPOTwentyTwo{} while accuracy on the remaining responses falls from \EOneRestGRPOTwentyTwoEarly{} to \EOneRestGRPOTwentyTwoEnd{}; seed 33's share at the limit rises from \EOneLimitGRPOThirtyThreeEarly{} to \EOneLimitGRPOThirtyThree{} while accuracy on the remaining responses falls from \EOneRestGRPOThirtyThreeEarly{} to \EOneRestGRPOThirtyThreeEnd{}.
Holding accuracy on terminating responses fixed at its rollout-200 level, increased non-termination accounts for \EOneLimitShareOfFallTwentyTwo{} of the overall drop in seed 22 and \EOneLimitShareOfFallThirtyThree{} in seed 33; because non-terminating responses are not a random subset of prompts, this accounting decomposes the observed score drop rather than isolating a causal mechanism.

\paragraph{A DRPO arm added after the first GRPO seed.}
After GRPO seed 11's internal validation accuracy fell from a peak of \EOneValidGRPOElevenPeak{} to \EOneValidGRPOElevenEnd{} at rollout 800---below its initial \EOneValidGRPOElevenStart{}---we added a DRPO recipe~\citep{yao2026rethinking} (configured as \texttt{spo\_adaptive\_eps}) under the same compute budget, dataset, hardware allocation, and seed IDs.
Adding this arm departs from the prespecified protocol, which registered only GRPO; the comparison plan between the two arms was recorded when DRPO seeds 22 and 33 were launched, before their outcomes were observed.
All three DRPO seeds completed 800 rollouts, and every saved DRPO checkpoint scores \EOneMathDRPOGainMin{} to \EOneMathDRPOGainMax{} percentage points above the frozen base on MATH-500.
Their final checkpoints score \EOneMathDRPOEndEleven{}, \EOneMathDRPOEndTwentyTwo{}, and \EOneMathDRPOEndThirtyThree{}, yielding a seed-level 95\% confidence interval of [\EOneMathDRPOChangeLow{}, \EOneMathDRPOChangeHigh{}] for the gain over base.
Under the default grader, every DRPO seed finishes above every GRPO seed---the strongest rank ordering possible with three seeds per arm ($p = \EOneArmRankP{}$, exact one-sided Mann--Whitney test over all $\binom{6}{3}=20$ assignments).
However, that final-checkpoint ordering hinges on how non-terminating responses are scored in one run.
In DRPO seed 22, \EOneLimitDRPOTwentyTwo{} of final MATH-500 answers reach the 16,384-token limit (versus at most \EOneLimitDRPOOthersMax{} in the other two DRPO seeds), yet \EOneLimitCorrectDRPOTwentyTwo{} of those truncated responses are graded correct because \texttt{math\_verify} accepts a boxed answer anywhere in the completion.
Under a stricter post-hoc scoring rule that counts every limit-reaching answer as incorrect, the final DRPO scores become \EOneStrictDRPOEleven{}, \EOneStrictDRPOTwentyTwo{}, and \EOneStrictDRPOThirtyThree{} versus \EOneStrictGRPOEleven{}, \EOneStrictGRPOTwentyTwo{}, and \EOneStrictGRPOThirtyThree{} for GRPO, placing DRPO seed 22 below the base model and yielding $p = \EOneStrictArmRankP{}$.
At rollouts 200, 400, and 600, every DRPO seed exceeds every GRPO run (including the seed 11 rerun) under either scoring rule (Table~\ref{tab:e1-limit}).
At rollouts 400 and 600, only \EOneLimitDRPOTwentyTwoMiddle{} and \EOneLimitDRPOTwentyTwoEarlier{} of DRPO seed 22's MATH-500 answers hit the 16,384-token evaluation limit, even though \EOneCapDRPOTwentyTwo{} of its training rollouts from rollout 301 onward reached the 8,192-token training cap (Appendix~\ref{app:e1}), because external evaluation permits twice the generation length at a lower decoding temperature.
Finally, the DRPO configuration differs from the GRPO configuration across five settings---the policy-gradient loss function, omission of group-standard-deviation advantage normalization, token-level loss aggregation, use of rollout-engine log probabilities as the $\pi_{\text{old}}$ anchor, and micro-batch shard balancing---so this comparison evaluates two complete recipes rather than isolating the objective alone.

\paragraph{E1 deviations and limits.}
In practice, seed IDs controlled only the training data order; rollout generation sampling was left unseeded, departing from the prespecified server-RNG seeding.
Evaluation decoding on the text benchmarks was also unseeded; consequently, the final checkpoints of GRPO seed 11 and DRPO seed 11 were each evaluated twice, and Table~\ref{tab:rq1-ar} reports their mean.
During AIME evaluation, the requested max-new-tokens budget was clipped to fit within the model's context window.
GRPO seed 11 and DRPO seed 22 were each resumed from their rollout-200 checkpoints, restoring model weights, optimizer state, and data-loader position (DRPO seed 22's initial allocation ended at rollout 369, and rollouts 201--369 from that first segment were discarded).
Because GRPO seed 11's checkpoints at rollouts 200 and 400 were lost from storage prior to evaluation, we launched a rerun under the same commit, dataset, and data order, scoring its checkpoints at rollouts 200 and 400 (\EOneMathGRPORerunEarly{} and \EOneMathGRPORerunMiddle{} on MATH-500) before resuming it from rollout 400 toward 800; that allocation ended at rollout \EOneRerunEnd{}, prior to checkpoint 600, leaving no later endpoint score.
Because generation sampling is unseeded, this rerun is an independent trajectory rather than an exact replay: its internal validation accuracy lies above the original seed 11 at every evaluation from rollout \EOneRerunAboveFrom{} to \EOneRerunAboveTo{} and below it from rollout \EOneRerunBelowFrom{} onward, finishing at \EOneValidRerunLast{} at rollout \EOneRerunLastEval{}---the lowest of the four GRPO runs at that step (Appendix~\ref{app:e1}).
We therefore report the rerun in a separate row in Table~\ref{tab:rq1-ar} and do not connect it to seed 11's later checkpoints.
Because no external reference framework was run to rollout 800 and evaluated on MATH-500 (E3-AR in Section~\ref{sec:rq2} runs for 500 rollouts without external benchmark scoring), we cannot isolate whether the late-training GRPO degradation stems from the recipe or from the system implementation.
The required training/evaluation overlap audit identified \EOneOverlapMath{} of the 500 MATH-500 problems among the \EOneTrainPrompts{} training prompts (\EOneOverlapMathVerbatim{} verbatim after normalization and \EOneOverlapMathNear{} near-verbatim) and zero overlapping AIME problems; excluding those \EOneOverlapMath{} problems alters no checkpoint's change from base by more than \EOneOverlapShiftMax{}.
An additional \EOneOverlapTemplate{} MATH-500 problems share at least half of their normalized 30-character substrings with a training prompt and were retained.
Finally, Appendix~\ref{app:e1} details the internal AIME validation series (evaluated every ten rollouts under training decoding settings) and the checkpoints it selects, along with token-limit truncation breakdowns, training diagnostics, and aborted launches excluded from Table~\ref{tab:rq1-ar}.

\paragraph{Diffusion workload.}
E2 fixes SD3.5-Medium~\citep{sd35medium2024} and the UniRL Flow-GRPO setting used by E3, i.e.\ the stock \codepath{sd3\_vllmomni.yaml} recipe with the alignment overrides in \codepath{run\_unirl\_sd35\_aligned.sh}: 48 prompts with 16 images (768 samples) per rollout, $384\times384$ training resolution, ten denoising steps, three SDE steps in the first half, $\eta=0.8$, guidance 1, and distinct initial noise.
PickScore~\citep{kirstain2023pickapic} supplies the training reward; advantages subtract each prompt-group mean and divide by the batch-wide reward standard deviation.
LoRA rank/alpha is 32/64 on eight matched attention projections; the learner uses two updates, micro-batch size eight, learning rate and weight decay $10^{-4}$, clip $10^{-5}$, and weight publication every rollout.
We prespecify 300 rollouts on one eight-GPU node with seed IDs 11, 22, and 33.
The same budget and seed IDs apply to the pinned VeRL-Omni reference run (E2-R); checkpoint export and estimator parity are audited before admission.
Because the stock configurations differ in SDE transition strategy---UniRL uses \texttt{FlowSDEStrategy} whereas VeRL-Omni uses Coefficients-Preserving Sampling (CPS)---as well as step selection and normalization/anchor semantics, E2 and E2-R currently define a related-recipe comparison. At least one full-budget E1 or E2 reference must pass mathematical and work parity before the paper claims reference-implementation reproduction.
Frozen base and every 50-rollout checkpoint use a shared diffusers evaluation pipeline. The primary image endpoint is PartiPrompts HPSv3~\citep{ma2025hpsv3}; ImageReward~\citep{xu2023imagereward} and compositional scores provide complementary checks.
These three metrics use held-fixed automatic judges distinct from the optimized PickScore model rather than human evaluations; Table~\ref{tab:rq1} and Figure~\ref{fig:e2-reported} also report held-out PickScore on PartiPrompts to track progress on the training objective itself.

\paragraph{Image evaluation identity.}
The registry key \texttt{image/geneval2} points to UniRL's \texttt{synthetic/test.jsonl} (800 prompts) and a Qwen3-VL-8B Soft-TIFA-style compositional VQA scorer, served by the reward service under the \texttt{vqascore} endpoint key by default or run in-process with \texttt{--local-geneval2}.
We refer to this benchmark as the \emph{synthetic compositional set} (labeled \texttt{Composition (VQAScore)} in Table~\ref{tab:rq1} and Figure~\ref{fig:e2-reported}); it is not an official GenEval~2 benchmark~\citep{kamath2025geneval2} without a separate data-and-scorer parity audit.
All image evaluations generate $512\times512$ images over 40 denoising steps with guidance scale 1.0 and base evaluation seed 42 shared across training seeds and checkpoints.
The PartiPrompts preference metrics (HPSv3, ImageReward, and PickScore) were evaluated on 16 images per prompt across all 1,632 prompts---matching the sample count required by the LPIPS diversity guard---rather than the prespecified single image per prompt.
Because image seeds are indexed deterministically by prompt and sample index independently of the total sample count, the prespecified single-image endpoint corresponds to the first-sample subset of these generations, which has not yet been scored separately.
The synthetic compositional endpoint uses one image per prompt (800 images), an explicit linear sigma grid, prompt-hashed seeds, and a 256-token text-encoder limit, whereas the PartiPrompts preference endpoint uses the pinned pipeline schedule and index-based seeds.
Dataset sizes, revisions, overlap counts, and scorer hashes remain open manifest fields.

The diversity guard is within-prompt mean pairwise LPIPS~\citep{zhang2018lpips} (AlexNet features), computed over 16 images per PartiPrompts prompt at the same evaluation setting, with identical image seeds for base and checkpoint.
We average all 120 pair distances per prompt, then report the relative change in the mean across prompts and a paired prompt-bootstrap interval.
A greater than 10\% relative decrease is a study-specific investigation trigger; it is not a literature-established quality threshold. A near-zero base makes this relative criterion undefined and requires the absolute change to be reported.
This guard diagnoses reduced diversity and is not itself a perceptual-quality metric.

\subsection{RQ3: What does the trajectory abstraction cost and enable?}
\label{sec:rq3}

RQ3 first tests the semantic capability that motivates the IR (E4, comprising synthetic equality verification E4a and three-seed training comparison E4b), and then measures its representation and topology costs (E5, comprising the paired representation microbenchmark E5-R and the deployment-topology comparison E5-T).
E4 uses the executable frozen-Qwen3-0.6B rewrite $\rightarrow$ SD3.5-Medium path: eight original prompts, four rewrites per prompt, and eight images per rewrite, or 256 image descendants per rollout; only diffusion trains.
This is a separate recipe from E2: $512\times512$, ten steps, one sampled SDE index among 0--8, $\eta=0.7$, LoRA 16/32, learning rate $3\times10^{-4}$, weight decay zero, clip $10^{-4}$, replay anchor, two updates, and micro-batch size one.
The primary condition, which we refer to as \emph{root scope} (\texttt{diffusion\_group\_scope=prompt}), computes group-relative advantages across all 32 image descendants of each original root prompt, whereas \emph{rewrite scope} (\texttt{diffusion\_group\_scope=rewrite}, the trainer default) normalizes across the eight images generated under each individual rewrite.
Because the two stock prompt-enhancement recipes are not otherwise matched---the stock prompt-scope file specifies eight images per rewrite and a 512-token rewrite limit, whereas the stock rewrite-scope file specifies four images and 256 tokens---both arms use the prompt-scope recipe with only the grouping key varied, as verified in their resolved configurations (with \texttt{shuffle\_updates} enabled in both arms).
Both conditions serialize redacted root, layer, and parent IDs, tensor shapes, rewards, advantages, replay indices, and group labels; because the synchronous prompt-enhancement trainer does not increment behavior-policy versions, serialized layers carry no behavior-version provenance in this workflow.
Invalid lineage remains an E0 fail-fast check rather than a corrupted training baseline.

For image $x_{p,j,k}$, the two group keys are $g_{\rm root}=p$ and $g_{\rm rewrite}=(p,j)$.
The semantic verification test (E4a) requires an explicit ID-joined flat-row reference to reproduce UniRL's group memberships, advantages, and learner inputs on identical recorded rewards and replay tensors, including across root-shard split/gather round trips.
Passing that recorded-trajectory control establishes that the IR implements the intended grouping semantics on real rollout data, though it does not by itself imply higher downstream image quality.
Conversely, because the 300-rollout, three-seed training comparison (E4b) alters the advantage-normalization population (32 versus eight images), any quality difference reflects the choice of grouping objective rather than the overhead of the IR representation.
The current PickScore request is conditioned by the rewritten text (the default \texttt{prompt\_source: generation}; scoring against the root prompt with \texttt{prompt\_source: original} is available but was not used in E4), so a claim about preserving the user's original intent additionally requires lineage-keyed evaluation of every image against its root prompt.
We therefore report both rewrite-conditioned training reward and frozen HPSv3/ImageReward evaluations joined to each root prompt.
Evaluation generates held-out images at $512\times512$, 40 steps, and guidance 1 with the base and every 50-rollout diffusion checkpoint, using one fixed manifest of four frozen rewrites per root and eight image seeds per rewrite for both conditions.
Worker/adapter RNG initialization, deterministic train-side AR sampling, real trace dumping, and this held-out generation/evaluation path are E0 prerequisites for a multi-seed E4 result.
If the two correct groupings tie, the experiment still establishes semantic expressiveness but not a quality benefit.

\subsection{RQ4: Exploratory bounded-staleness observations}
\label{sec:rq4}

The prespecified E6 study uses the E1 Qwen3-4B-Base GRPO workload on DAPO-Math-17k across 32 GPUs (64 prompts $\times$ eight samples and four optimizer updates per rollout).
Its grid separates three factors: (i) device allocation, comparing colocated synchronous execution on all 32 GPUs (\texttt{S0}) against disaggregated 16-training/16-rollout execution with one batch in flight, weight publication after every batch, and zero batch-staleness allowance ($b=0$, so policy lag $\ell = 0$; \texttt{D0}); (ii) the onset of generation--training overlap, comparing \texttt{D0} against \texttt{A1} (two batches in flight, publication interval 1, staleness allowance $b=1$); and (iii) wider staleness allowances and publication intervals (\texttt{A2}: in-flight 2, interval 1, $b=2$; \texttt{A3}: in-flight 2, interval 2, $b=2$; \texttt{A4}: in-flight 2, interval 2, $b=4$).
The formal grid sweep was canceled after preliminary runs; only single-seed capacity measurements (with evaluation disabled) and a separate quality-target pilot on \texttt{D0} were completed.
In the single-seed capacity runs, reported per-rollout durations were $82.34$\,s for \texttt{S0}, $103.07$\,s for zero-overlap disaggregated \texttt{D0}, and $56.48$\,s (\texttt{A1}), $55.19$\,s (\texttt{A2}), $33.47$\,s (\texttt{A3}), and $39.54$\,s (\texttt{A4}, pooled over two replicates) once overlap was enabled.
Because those preliminary timings were collected at one seed with evaluation disabled---and before aligning five stock learner configuration differences between the synchronous and asynchronous recipes---they serve only as planning observations rather than a controlled speedup or equal-quality comparison.

\paragraph{The zero-lag quality-target pilot did not pass.}
Before running the overlapped grid for quality, the protocol required registering a target MATH-500 threshold from the disaggregated zero-overlap baseline \texttt{D0}.
In that pilot, \texttt{D0}'s reported MATH-500 avg@4 decreased from \ESixQualityEarly{} at rollout 150 to \ESixQualityLate{} at rollout 300, contrary to the prespecified requirement that the later checkpoint improve, and training reward declined over the same window.
No quality threshold was registered and no time-to-quality result is reported.
Because \texttt{D0} enforces one batch in flight, weight publication after every batch, and zero allowed policy lag ($b = 0 \Rightarrow \ell = 0$, so generation and training strictly alternate without staleness; Section~\ref{sec:async}), its degradation reflects the training horizon and learner dynamics of the zero-lag baseline rather than policy staleness.
Because no configuration with $b > 0$ ($\ell > 0$) or generation--training overlap was evaluated for quality before cancellation, E6 provides no empirical evidence on how bounded staleness affects model quality.
Two checkpoints are also insufficient to locate a precise turning point.

\paragraph{Evaluation variability and requirements for a restart.}
Repeated evaluation of a single checkpoint exhibited noticeable prompt-level avg@4 variance despite a small aggregate difference.
That observation is specific to the sampled checkpoint and decoding protocol; it is not a universal noise floor or a substitute for training-seed replication.
A renewed study would first audit scorer behavior, request-level sampling, and repeat-evaluation variability; match the synchronous/asynchronous estimator and learner settings; and record complete driver time and buffer admission, rejection, discard, retry, and completion accounting.
The trainer's internal \texttt{perf/step\_time\_s} metric alone omits outer-loop weight publication, evaluation, and checkpoint work.
Only after establishing a suitable learning budget and a separately piloted target should a replicated formal grid support a lag--quality--time conclusion.

\subsection{Fairness and statistical protocol}

Effective work is matched before wall-clock time: prompts, samples per prompt, decoded resolution or maximum tokens, rollout steps, replayed steps/tokens, micro-batching semantics, and optimizer updates.
Primary full-system metrics count all allocated training, rollout, and reward resources on both sides. Reward placement and concurrency must be disclosed; a reward-excluded number is supplementary and carries a separate service-cost report.
We prespecify warm-up removal, use at least 20 steady-state iterations for throughput summaries, and report the full distribution rather than a single minimum.
Training summaries use the training seed as the primary statistical unit. Across E1, E2, and E4, individual seed trajectories or endpoint scores are shown in Table~\ref{tab:rq1-ar} and Figure~\ref{fig:e1-endpoint} (E1), Figure~\ref{fig:e2-reported} (E2), and Table~\ref{tab:e4-grouping} (E4), while seed-level 95\% $t$ intervals (with two degrees of freedom for three seeds, and thus wide by construction) accompany the three-seed endpoint changes in Table~\ref{tab:rq1} and the paired scope contrast in Table~\ref{tab:e4-grouping}. Bootstrap intervals over prompts or images are labeled separately and do not substitute for cross-seed uncertainty.
The complete systems release must show all process repetitions and the post-warm-up per-rollout distribution; uncertainty of a speed ratio must use repetitions, not correlated rollouts, as its independent units.
E3-AR releases its 499-rollout post-warm-up distribution, but has only one process-level run per framework; the older report-derived E3 summaries provide neither the complete per-rollout distribution nor independently revalidated repetitions.
The final checkpoint and named primary endpoint are fixed in advance; the auxiliary metrics diagnose failure modes and are not alternative endpoints chosen after seeing results.
Any future E6 time-to-quality study must use MATH-500 and a target prespecified from a separate pilot before the formal grid, bracket first observed crossings by evaluation times, and censor runs that never cross. The canceled study produced no such result.
Elapsed time spans initialization, training, publication, checkpointing, and evaluation; steady-state throughput is reported separately.
All published cells require complete scoring or an explicit missingness analysis; silently averaging only successful evaluator responses is inadmissible.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{LLM post-training systems.}
HybridFlow/veRL combines single- and multi-controller execution and introduces hierarchical APIs for mapping RLHF dataflows to devices~\citep{sheng2024hybridflow}.
OpenRLHF uses Ray, vLLM, and DeepSpeed to schedule the models in an RLHF workflow~\citep{hu2024openrlhf}.
ROLL emphasizes a single-controller abstraction, parallel workers, sample lifecycle scheduling, and flexible device mapping~\citep{wang2025roll}.
NeMo-RL provides scalable training and generation backends and multimodal model support~\citep{nemorl2025}.
Group-relative recipes in these systems~\citep{sheng2024hybridflow,hu2024openrlhf,wang2025roll,nemorl2025} typically record one level of branching---responses grouped under their prompt by a prompt identifier or by contiguous row order---whereas UniRL generalizes that group key to multi-level ancestry encoded in trajectory-node identifiers (\texttt{sample\_id} and \texttt{parent\_id} within a \sample{}).
These systems already drive distinct generation and training backends from one control loop and colocate or separate those backends as a placement choice; UniRL retains that execution split while replacing flat row batches with lineage-preserving \sample{} trees as the scheduled unit.
Scheduling and DP sharding move complete root groups rather than rows, and each generated layer carries the replay segment for its own AR or diffusion stage (Section~\ref{sec:trajectory}).
A flat-row batch can recover the same group memberships by joining on retained parent keys (Section~\ref{sec:problem}); UniRL differs in that sharding, credit assignment, and cross-stage conditioning consume that ancestry in place, so changing the execution path does not require rebuilding the join.
RQ3 specifies the corresponding control: an ID-joined flat-row reference over recorded rewards and replay tensors (Section~\ref{sec:rq3}).
To date, that reference check covers group membership and advantages on recorded training rollouts (E4~v2), whereas replay tensors, learner inputs, and root-shard round trips are verified only on synthetic fixtures (E4a); the paired representation cost of carrying tree structure (E5-R) remains unmeasured beyond single-process CPU regression timings.

\paragraph{Asynchronous post-training.}
AReaL fully decouples generation and training and controls staleness to improve language-reasoning throughput~\citep{fu2025areal}.
Relax uses service-level separation, a TransferQueue data bus, and configurable staleness for omni-modal post-training~\citep{zhang2026relax}.
slime supports persistent asynchronous rollout generation around a training/rollout/data-buffer loop~\citep{slime2025}.
Within the asynchronous design space explored by these systems, UniRL adopts a conservative operating point in which weight publication remains a synchronization barrier and policy lag is bounded in optimizer-update units (Section~\ref{sec:async}).
Each generated layer records its behavior-policy version in the trajectory IR, while buffer admission, root-complete lag filtering, and publication barriers are coordinated by a shared asynchronous rollout manager across \texttt{AsyncARTrainer} and \texttt{AsyncDiffusionTrainer}.
The filter keeps or drops whole root groups so that group-relative baselines stay complete, and no training batch spans behavior versions; this is stricter than AReaL, whose decoupled objective admits trajectories generated under several behavior versions~\citep{fu2025areal}.
RQ4 reports only single-seed capacity measurements and a quality-target pilot that did not pass; it supports no throughput or time-to-quality comparison with these systems (Section~\ref{sec:rq4}).

\paragraph{Multimodal and diffusion post-training systems.}
VeRL-Omni extends veRL toward diffusion, unified, and omni-modality models and integrates vLLM-Omni rollout~\citep{verlomni2026}.
Relax is explicitly omni-native~\citep{zhang2026relax}.
Rather than modality coverage alone, what distinguishes UniRL from these systems is how ancestry and typed replay segments survive execution: an AR stage and a diffusion stage are represented as successive layers of a single \sample{} (Section~\ref{sec:trajectory}), and tensor references transport their dense payloads without altering that lineage (Section~\ref{sec:execution}).
Because model bundles and \texttt{StageAlgorithm} implementations interact only through typed stage conditions, replay segments, and replayed sufficient statistics, supporting $N_{\mathrm{mod}}$ model bundles and $N_{\mathrm{alg}}$ shared algorithms requires $N_{\mathrm{mod}}+N_{\mathrm{alg}}$ rather than $N_{\mathrm{mod}}\times N_{\mathrm{alg}}$ integrations (Section~\ref{sec:algorithm-boundary}).
E4 exercises this boundary by coupling a frozen AR prompt-rewrite stage to a trained diffusion stage (Section~\ref{sec:rq3}).
Its lineage check verifies group membership and advantages on recorded training rollouts (E4~v2) and replay-tensor propagation on synthetic fixtures (E4a), while its three-seed quality comparison between root-level and rewrite-level grouping is inconclusive.
A static module inventory can document which trajectory, transport, and credit components this pipeline reuses, though it does not measure human engineering effort.

\paragraph{Diffusion RL algorithms.}
DDPO casts denoising as a multi-step decision process~\citep{black2023ddpo}.
Flow-GRPO converts flow sampling to an SDE and applies group-relative optimization~\citep{liu2025flowgrpo}; DanceGRPO generalizes related ideas across visual generation tasks~\citep{xue2025dancegrpo}; DiffusionNFT instead optimizes a forward-process objective from reward-ranked generations~\citep{zheng2025diffusionnft}.
UniRL implements Flow-GRPO-style and DiffusionNFT objectives behind its \texttt{StageAlgorithm} interface without claiming either objective as an algorithmic contribution (Section~\ref{sec:instantiations}).
Instead, the interface keeps their distinct sufficient statistics explicit: Flow-GRPO-style updates replay recorded reverse-SDE transitions, whereas DiffusionNFT evaluates forward-process noising losses on terminal generated latents, while both share the same \texttt{TrainStack} for micro-batching and optimizer state.

\section{Limitations and Open Evidence}
\label{sec:limitations}

\paragraph{Integration cost is per architecture.}
Adding an architecture requires one bundle with its stages, conditions, and rollout adapters; this cost is paid once per model rather than once per model--algorithm pair.
Architecture-specific details such as tokenizers, noise schedulers, latent layouts, and LoRA targets stay inside that bundle and its adapters rather than being abstracted by the trajectory type.
The ordered-layer representation targets trajectories that grow by forking and observation; arbitrary ragged forests are outside its current scope.

\paragraph{Topology is configured, not optimized.}
Placement scopes make device allocation explicit but do not automatically search for an optimal resource split across roles, which depends on model memory footprint, reward-evaluation latency, rollout length variance, interconnect bandwidth, and weight-publication volume.

\paragraph{Asynchronous execution is bounded and partially specialized.}
Weight publication is a barrier, and current AR and diffusion paths impose different admissible in-flight structure.
Fault isolation is limited by the shared Ray runtime and role lifecycle; the system does not yet claim service-level recovery comparable to dedicated service architectures.

\paragraph{Case-study scope.}
The reward-serving and rollout-fidelity case studies (Sections~\ref{sec:reward} and~\ref{sec:fidelity}) evaluate targeted mechanisms outside the E0--E6 program on a single BAGEL image-editing workload with small rollout counts per arm.
In Section~\ref{sec:reward-results}, the image-editing wall-clock durations are rescaled estimates that combine measured reward-side traces with the generation speedup from restored source conditioning; in Section~\ref{sec:reward-mps}, the MPS colocation numbers come from single-GPU scorer qualification benchmarks rather than end-to-end training runs, and the dynamic load balancer in Section~\ref{sec:fidelity-tail} is evaluated in a standalone two-rollout microbenchmark.

\paragraph{The empirical case is incomplete.}
The GPU experiments support only qualified, workload-specific observations: (i)~diffusion post-training (E2) improves held-out automatic scores while reducing within-prompt diversity; (ii)~autoregressive training (E1) exhibits early gains followed by late-stage degradation under the prespecified GRPO recipe, whereas a post-hoc DRPO recipe improves over the base model as graded (with one seed whose final-checkpoint answers largely fail to terminate); (iii)~the composed rewrite-to-image pipeline (E4~v2) computes both lineage-defined groupings accurately and trains above the base model; and (iv)~the systems experiments measure native-recipe iteration time (E3), colocated-versus-separate deployment sensitivity (E5-T), and single-run output-token throughput in a recipe-matched AR comparison (E3-AR).
Because E3-AR comprises a single process-level run per framework without external checkpoint evaluation, it does not establish run-to-run variance, training-quality parity, or time-to-quality.
More broadly, the current evidence does not establish mathematically aligned reference reproduction (E2-R), endpoint improvement under the prespecified AR recipe (E1), a quality advantage from root-level over rewrite-level grouping (E4~v2), bounded IR representation overhead (E5-R), multi-node scaling, or a time-to-quality benefit from asynchronous execution (E6).
The E3, E5-T, and E6 summaries remain derived from legacy experiment reports: their raw GPU logs are unavailable, and they still lack complete run manifests, reconciled replicate provenance, and independent regeneration.
The E0, E1, E2, and E4 tables, figures, and quoted statistics are generated directly from exported per-run evaluation and rollout records in the repository, but their underlying completions, generated images, full runtime logs, and checkpoint weights remain on cluster storage outside the released bundle.
Furthermore, while E4~v2 validates group membership and advantage computation on recorded rollouts, it neither archives the validation outputs in the bundle nor checks replay tensors on recorded payloads, and its three-seed quality contrast remains statistically inconclusive.
The late-stage GRPO degradation also remains unexplained: no matched external reference implementation was evaluated on the E1 protocol, and the DRPO configuration differs from the GRPO configuration across five simultaneous settings, preventing attribution to the policy loss alone.
Moreover, the final-checkpoint rank ordering between DRPO and GRPO holds only under default \texttt{math\_verify} grading and disappears if completions that reach the 16,384-token generation limit are scored as incorrect.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

UniRL formulates post-training around a single trajectory representation that persists across generative model families and execution topologies.
Ancestry, decoded multimodal payloads, stage-specific replay segments, rewards, advantages, and behavior-policy versions reside in one trajectory IR, while rollout, reward evaluation, and training operate as logical roles connected by explicit placement, transport, and weight-publication contracts.
Two case studies on instruction-guided image editing illustrate how these boundaries operate in practice: micro-batched generation--reward overlap hides remote judge latency when the judge has spare capacity, while structural token-layout checks and sibling request packing across the rollout-engine boundary recover conditioning fidelity and reduce generation time.
Across the main evaluation program, our experiments show (i)~held-out diffusion score gains accompanied by reduced within-prompt diversity (E2), (ii)~early autoregressive learning followed by late-stage degradation below the base model under the prespecified GRPO recipe, alongside positive final-checkpoint gains under a post-hoc DRPO recipe across all three seeds as graded (E1), (iii)~exact agreement with an ID-joined reference on recorded rollouts and positive training gains under both lineage-defined groupings in the composed rewrite-to-image workflow (E4~v2), and (iv)~lower native-recipe diffusion iteration time than VeRL-Omni (E3), a substantial iteration-time penalty for disaggregated diffusion deployment (E5-T), and 8.2\% higher end-to-end output-token throughput than veRL in a single recipe-matched Qwen3-4B-Base GRPO run (E3-AR).
That AR systems result is a workload-specific throughput observation rather than a repeated-run or time-to-quality conclusion.
The full trajectory-centric claim remains open until replay-level lineage validation on recorded payloads and matched representation-cost measurements complement those observations, and bounded staleness remains an exploratory operating-region question with no demonstrated time-to-quality benefit in the canceled study.

\paragraph{Training diagnostics.}
Figure~\ref{fig:e1-training} plots the exported per-rollout E1 training diagnostics (the policy-loss and wall-clock series specified in the protocol were not exported).
Across all three formal GRPO seeds between the first 100 rollouts and the last 100 rollouts, mean response length increases from \EOneLenGRPOEarlyMin{}--\EOneLenGRPOEarlyMax{} to \EOneLenGRPOLateMin{}--\EOneLenGRPOLateMax{} tokens, the fraction of completions reaching the 8,192-token training cap rises from \EOneCapGRPOEarlyMin{}--\EOneCapGRPOEarlyMax{} to \EOneCapGRPOLateMin{}--\EOneCapGRPOLateMax{}, and the fraction of zero-variance prompt groups (where all eight samples receive identical reward and contribute zero gradient) rises from \EOneZeroGRPOEarlyMin{}--\EOneZeroGRPOEarlyMax{} to \EOneZeroGRPOLateMin{}--\EOneZeroGRPOLateMax{}.
Meanwhile, step-size diagnostics remain small: across 25-rollout blocks in all six formal runs, the median approximate KL divergence is at most \EOneKLMedianLargest{}, the median maximum importance ratio is at most \EOneRatioMedianLargest{}, and no formal GRPO rollout clips more than \EOneClipLargest{} of its tokens, although isolated rollouts spike up to an approximate KL of \EOneKLLargest{} and a maximum importance ratio of \EOneRatioLargest{}.
The GRPO degradation thus coincides with response lengthening, increased truncation, and shrinking within-group reward variance rather than importance-ratio instability or excessive clipping; however, the DRPO runs reach even longer mean response lengths (Figure~\ref{fig:e1-training}b), showing that response growth alone does not explain why GRPO degrades while DRPO improves.

\paragraph{Discarded launches and checkpoint provenance.}
The following aborted or superseded segments are excluded from Table~\ref{tab:rq1-ar}: three initial launches of GRPO seed 11 that failed prior to rollout 1; an initial launch of GRPO seed 22 stopped at rollout 19 when its compute allocation was reassigned to the DRPO arm; an initial launch of DRPO seed 11 that ran for 39 rollouts with identically zero gradients before overriding \texttt{rl\_on\_policy\_target=null} (an override retained by all three reported DRPO runs); and rollouts 201--369 from DRPO seed 22's first allocation prior to its resumption from checkpoint 200.
The GRPO seed 11 rerun executed the formal 800-rollout launch command, was stopped after saving and exporting checkpoint 400, and was then resumed from checkpoint 400 under the same command until its allocation expired at rollout \EOneRerunEnd{} (prior to checkpoint 600); in the exported training series, the single post-400 rollout logged before the stop is replaced by the resumed segment's rollout, and the duplicate initial validation evaluation logged upon checkpoint restoration is omitted.
Finally, each external benchmark evaluation ran against a bf16 export of the saved checkpoint without recording a cryptographic hash of the exported weights, so the bundle identifies evaluated checkpoints by run ID and rollout step alone.
