\begin{abstract}
Reinforcement-learning post-training for generative models follows a common semantic loop---generate, score, assign credit, and update---yet existing systems fragment across autoregressive, diffusion, and multi-stage multimodal workflows, which require different replay state, lineage tracking, and execution topologies.
We present UniRL, a trajectory-centric system that places a lineage-preserving, modality-aware intermediate representation (IR) at the boundary between post-training semantics and distributed execution.
Each trajectory is a prompt-rooted tree of typed generation layers that records each layer's decoded outputs, conditioning inputs, stage-specific replay segment, rewards, and behavior-policy version.
Three logical roles operate on this representation---rollout engines populate generation layers, reward and credit operators evaluate and fold feedback along tree ancestry, and training backends replay stage-specific objectives---while explicit runtime contracts govern device placement, tensor transport, weight publication, and memory residency.
These interfaces decouple model-specific replay from lineage tracking, data movement, and scheduling, without forcing heterogeneous objectives or engine--topology pairs into a monolithic trainer.
We implement this design in UniRL v0.2.0, validate ten core IR invariants in a source-importing CPU harness, and examine its reward-serving and rollout-engine boundaries through two image-editing case studies on generation--reward overlap and conditioning fidelity.
Three-seed diffusion post-training improves held-out automatic scores together with reduced within-prompt diversity, while an eight-GPU native-recipe comparison reports lower iteration time than VeRL-Omni.
In a recipe-matched 500-rollout Qwen3-4B-Base Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) comparison, UniRL records 8.2\% higher end-to-end output-token throughput than veRL on runs executed concurrently on separate cluster nodes, with UniRL sampling 11.0\% longer responses and a higher 8,192-token truncation rate (0.307 vs.\ 0.203).
On the primary autoregressive endpoint, the prespecified GRPO recipe learns early and then degrades, ending below the base model in all three seeds, while a DRPO recipe added later ends above it in all three seeds as graded, one of them only because the grader credits answers that run to the token limit.
In a composed rewrite-to-image run, two lineage-defined groupings match an ID-joined reference on recorded rollouts and both train; their quality comparison is inconclusive.
The present evaluation distinguishes values generated from exported run outputs and the rerun AR systems comparison from legacy report-derived summaries: the cause of the GRPO degradation, replay-level lineage validation on recorded payloads, paired representation cost, and a controlled time-to-quality result remain open.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Online post-training is a feedback program.
It expands inputs into policy samples, evaluates those samples, turns feedback into a learning signal, and applies one or more optimizer updates.
At this level, language-model Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), diffusion-model policy gradients, prompt-rewrite pipelines, and tool-using agents look deceptively similar.
Their data paths do not.
An autoregressive (AR) update may need packed token IDs, masks, rollout log probabilities, and per-token values; a diffusion update may need a sparse set of latent states, a noise schedule, and transition statistics; a composed workflow must retain which image descended from which rewritten prompt; an asynchronous run must additionally retain which behavior version generated every sample.

Existing systems have made major progress on scalable LLM post-training and flexible device mapping~\citep{sheng2024hybridflow,hu2024openrlhf,wang2025roll,nemorl2025}.
Fully or partially asynchronous systems further address rollout tail latency and accelerator idle time~\citep{fu2025areal,zhang2026relax,slime2025}.
Post-training is now expanding from single-stage text generation to iterative diffusion denoising and multi-stage multimodal pipelines~\citep{black2023ddpo,liu2025flowgrpo,xue2025dancegrpo,zheng2025diffusionnft,verlomni2026}.
Those stages differ in modality, replay state, and branching factor while still sharing ancestry for conditioning and credit assignment, so flat LLM-specific tensor dictionaries no longer suffice.
The remaining design question is how the information shared by these workflows should survive changes in their model-specific state and execution paths.

\begin{quote}
\emph{Can one post-training program cross generative model families and execution topologies while retaining the structure needed for correct grouping, replay, credit assignment, transport, and behavior-version control?}
\end{quote}

Our answer in UniRL is to introduce a \emph{trajectory intermediate representation (IR)}---a representation for stages that differ in modality and batch size---and expose execution choices as explicit contracts around that representation.
Rather than treating an individual row or an LLM-specific tensor dictionary as the unit of scheduling and sharding, the IR organizes each batch as a forest of prompt-rooted trees whose width grows when a stage branches.
Within each layer, typed leaves separate decoded multimodal values, model conditions, and per-stage replay segments.
Distributed methods declare how each structured field scatters, concatenates, packs, or remains shared, and dense tensors can be replaced by row-addressable references without changing the trajectory structure.
This representation flows through a small set of logical roles that can be mapped onto colocated or separate \emph{GPU slabs} (contiguous subsets of accelerators allocated to a role).

The paper develops three coupled design ideas:

\begin{itemize}
  \item A \emph{lineage-preserving trajectory IR} in which a rollout request is a typed tree skeleton spanning AR and diffusion stages, each generated layer is conditioned through its ancestry and keeps its own replay segment, and training folds reward and credit back along the same tree (Sections~\ref{sec:trajectory-layers}--\ref{sec:fold}). Synthetic equality checks and an ID-join check on recorded rollouts of a composed run support the grouping semantics; the quality comparison between the two groupings is inconclusive, and replay-level validation on recorded payloads remains open.
  \item A \emph{modular interface for stage replay and optimization} that separates trajectory layers from stage-specific losses and distributed training stacks, so $N_{\mathrm{mod}}$ model bundles and $N_{\mathrm{alg}}$ shared algorithms require $N_{\mathrm{mod}}+N_{\mathrm{alg}}$ integrations rather than $N_{\mathrm{mod}}\times N_{\mathrm{alg}}$ (Sections~\ref{sec:algorithm-boundary} and~\ref{sec:instantiations}).
  \item A \emph{factorization of semantic roles from physical execution}: rollout remains one $\sample\!\rightarrow\!\sample$ fill of the trajectory skeleton, while field-aware dispatch, tensor references, placement, weight publication, and memory residency govern how those roles run on colocated, separate, and asynchronous topologies (Section~\ref{sec:execution}), with two case studies examining reward-serving isolation/overlap and rollout-engine conditioning fidelity (Sections~\ref{sec:reward} and~\ref{sec:fidelity}). Native-recipe, recipe-matched AR, and internal topology comparisons provide initial performance observations; the paired representation-cost control remains unmeasured.
\end{itemize}

Asynchronous execution is where the third idea pays off most directly.
Because rollout, reward, and training are logical roles rather than fixed steps of one loop, the orchestration layer---admission, root-complete grouping, version stamping, lag filtering, and weight publication---can be lifted out of individual trainers.
\system's AR and diffusion trainers therefore share one bounded-staleness manager over the same trajectory IR in code (Section~\ref{sec:async}).
Empirically, our exploratory bounded-staleness study exercises only the Qwen3-4B-Base AR workload: single-seed planning runs with evaluation disabled record lower per-rollout time once generation--training overlap is enabled, but the zero-lag disaggregated quality-target pilot degraded between rollouts 150 and 300 and the formal sweep was canceled, so no asynchronous time-to-quality or diffusion-asynchrony result is claimed (Section~\ref{sec:rq4}).

The evidence is organized from structural correctness, through credible learning and a composed workflow, to measured system cost.
The experiment IDs and corresponding artifacts are specified in Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}.

\section{Problem: Preserve Semantics While Changing Execution}
\label{sec:problem}

Let an online post-training iteration consume a batch of initial root inputs $X_0$, such as text prompts or multimodal contexts, sample trajectories with behavior policy $\pi_v$, evaluate rewards $R$ via reward operator $\mathcal{R}$, construct credit $A$ via credit-assignment operator $\mathcal{G}$, and update parameters $\theta$:
\begin{equation}
X_0 \xrightarrow{\pi_v} \mathcal{T}
  \xrightarrow{\mathcal{R}} (\mathcal{T},R)
  \xrightarrow{\mathcal{G}} (\mathcal{T},A)
  \xrightarrow{U_\theta} \theta'.
\label{eq:outer-loop}
\end{equation}
The compact notation hides three forms of heterogeneity.

\paragraph{Trajectory heterogeneity.}
The sufficient statistics for an update depend on the generative process.
For AR policies, a replay segment may be a variable-length packed token sequence with masks, old log probabilities, values, and returns.
For stochastic differential equation (SDE) diffusion or flow policies, a replay segment may contain selected latent snapshots, a shared noise schedule, the indices of sampled transitions, and rollout or replay transition statistics.
Multi-stage and agentic paths add nonuniform lineage and observations.

For example, let prompt $p$ produce rewrites $w_{p,j}$ and images $x_{p,j,k}$.
The image model conditions on $w_{p,j}$, but credit may compare either images under that rewrite or all images under $p$.
A flattened image batch can implement both choices only if it retains or reconstructs the relevant parent keys.
This example motivates explicit ancestry, while the image model's replay state motivates typed replay segments; neither requirement follows from the other.

\paragraph{Role heterogeneity.}
Generation, reward evaluation, replay, gradient computation, and weight publication differ in runtime, parallel layout, and lifecycle, not only in kernels.
Rollout may run inside the training process or in a dedicated serving engine with its own tensor-parallel (TP) layout, such as SGLang~\citep{zheng2024sglang}, vLLM~\citep{kwon2023vllm}, or vLLM-Omni~\citep{yin2026vllmomni}, while a sharded training backend such as FSDP~\citep{zhao2023fsdp} or VeOmni~\citep{ma2025veomni} owns parameters and optimizer state.
Reward ranges from an in-process scorer to an environment-isolated scorer process or a remote multi-model service.
These roles must agree on when each copy of the policy is resident, offloaded, or published, and publishing from a sharded trainer is itself a collective over the whole training mesh.
Which boundary dominates cost depends on the configuration: in the measured separate-slab deployment, much of the slowdown arises from reward processing (Section~\ref{sec:rq3}).

\paragraph{Topology heterogeneity.}
A small run may time-share one GPU set between training and rollout; a larger run may separate them; a variable-duration rollout may benefit from asynchronous admission.
Each choice changes memory pressure, transfer paths, synchronization frequency, and allowable policy lag.

These observations produce four requirements (Table~\ref{tab:requirements}).
The first two follow from trajectory heterogeneity and the third from role and topology heterogeneity; the fourth specializes the third to asynchronous execution, where decoupled roles may train on work generated by an older policy.

\paragraph{Non-goals.}
UniRL does not erase model-specific code; it confines it to a model bundle---architecture-specific stages, conditions, and rollout adapters---and to an algorithm that defines its loss over replay.
It does not automatically discover an optimal placement.
The present runtime uses a driver to sequence roles and Ray actors~\citep{moritz2018ray} to host them; it is not a new cluster scheduler.

\section{A Trajectory-Centric Programming Model}
\label{sec:trajectory}

\subsection{A trajectory batch is a sequence of tree layers}
\label{sec:trajectory-layers}

The central type is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{T} = [P_0,P_1,\ldots,P_L],
\qquad
P_i = (I_i, X_i, C_i, Z_i, R_i, A_i, V_i, M_i),
\label{eq:sample}
\end{equation}
where $I_i$ are sample identifiers, $X_i$ decoded modality values, $C_i$ model conditions, $Z_i$ replay segments, $R_i$ rewards, $A_i$ advantages or returns, $V_i$ the generating engine's behavior version $v_b$ (advanced at weight publication on the asynchronous and agentic paths, and by vLLM's native weight-transfer engine; Section~\ref{sec:lifecycle}), and $M_i$ auxiliary metadata.
In code, $\mathcal{T}$ is a \sample{} and each layer $P_i$ is represented by the batched class \parttype{}; we refer to $P_i$ as a \emph{layer} throughout the paper.
The sequence encodes a forest of root-prompt trees: $P_i$ holds every node at depth $i$, and edges connect adjacent layers.
We call the last layer the \emph{frontier}; trajectories grow by appending layers to it.
A generated layer is typed by the sampling-parameter class that produced it and the replay segment class it carries, for example an AR layer with a \texttt{TextSegment} or a diffusion layer with a \texttt{LatentSegment}; input layers carry the prompts and observations that condition generation.

Identifiers encode ancestry.
Root IDs contain no lineage separator; a branch appends a child ordinal.
For every non-root layer $P_i$ ($i>0$) and every row identifier $s\in I_i$, its parent ID must occur in $P_{i-1}$:
\begin{equation}
\forall i>0,\;\forall s\in I_i:\quad \operatorname{parent}(s)\in I_{i-1}.
\label{eq:parent-invariant}
\end{equation}
The constructor checks Equation~\ref{eq:parent-invariant}.
The \texttt{fork} operator appends $k$ generated children per row of the frontier; \texttt{observe} appends a branch-one input from an environment; and \texttt{propagate\_rewards} reduces child reward to an unscored parent.

Batch operations act on complete root groups.
\texttt{Sample.split()} returns one tree-complete trajectory per root, and slicing or selection is implemented by selecting these groups rather than independent rows.
Moving complete roots preserves the parent-contiguous order constructed by \texttt{fork}; independently permuting child rows need not preserve the credit computation.

\paragraph{Well-formed batches.}
Lineage operations are defined over well-formed trajectory batches: root IDs are unique, trajectories being merged have the same number of layers, and siblings follow the parent-major order produced by \texttt{fork}.
The programming model establishes these properties by construction---trajectories grow only through \texttt{fork} and \texttt{observe}, and batches are sharded and selected as complete root groups---so credit assignment and reward reduction can consume each sibling group as one contiguous block.
Group-advantage computation rejects batches whose siblings are not contiguous, layer concatenation requires matching decoded-value metadata across shards, and the asynchronous batch assembler admits only batches generated by a single behavior version.
E0 is the gate that checks these properties at the actual experiment boundaries (Appendix~\ref{app:correctness}).

\subsection{Values, conditions, and segments are separate layers}

UniRL distinguishes three kinds of leaves that are often conflated.

\paragraph{Primitives.}
Primitives are decoded or externally supplied values: text, images, videos, audio waveforms, and media references.
Image pixels and variable-duration video/audio values use packed representations with per-example shapes or cumulative offsets.
They are suitable for reward functions, visualization, and subsequent turns, but need not be the tensors replayed by the trainable model.

\paragraph{Conditions.}
Conditions are model-facing encodings, such as text embeddings or image latents.
They allow rollout adapters and train-side stages to agree on typed inputs without prescribing one tokenizer, text encoder, or multimodal fusion architecture.

\paragraph{Segments.}
Segments retain stage-specific replay segments.
\texttt{TextSegment} stores variable-length packed token fields.
\texttt{LatentSegment} stores latent snapshots at selected indices, a shared sigma schedule, and optional SDE log probabilities or transition means.
The sparse index map is important: a diffusion algorithm can replay only selected transitions instead of materializing an entire denoising path for every loss.

The distinction makes decoded payload lifetime independent of replay lifetime.
After reward logging, trainers may drop large decoded media while retaining conditions, segments, reward, and credit for training.

\subsection{A field algebra defines structural batching}

Every structured batch field carries a kind: concatenated, packed variable-length, or shared (the default for an unannotated field); scalar reductions by maximum, minimum, sum, or mean are also available but are applied only when concatenating, and the current source uses only the maximum, for timing metadata.
Concatenation, row selection, slicing, and repetition act recursively on concatenated and packed fields and pass shared fields through unchanged; device movement applies to every tensor regardless of kind.
The same kinds define how a distributed method reconstructs its return value.
For example, a data-parallel (DP) scattered \sample{} is split by root groups (the root count must be divisible by the DP size), and the DP heads' results are concatenated position-wise; another argument whose leading size differs from the split size is replicated to every shard rather than rejected.
A broadcast lifecycle method returns the list of per-worker results, and callers that expect a replicated value check agreement explicitly.

This removes hand-authored tensor slicing from each role; merging shards relies on the well-formed-batch properties above.
It also creates a measurable cost: recursive structural operations and reference bookkeeping must be benchmarked in Section~\ref{sec:rq3} rather than assumed free.

\subsection{Rollout unfolds a declared trajectory tree}
\label{sec:unfold}

A rollout request is the skeleton of the tree it will become.
Starting from the root layer $P_0$, the caller appends one generated layer per stage with \texttt{fork}; until an engine fills its tensors, we refer to such an unfilled layer in a request skeleton as a \emph{shell}.
Each shell fixes its child count, carries child identifiers that already name their parents, and holds a typed sampling-parameter object---AR or diffusion---that identifies the generative process that must fill it.
The prompt-enhancement and unified-model trainers build
\begin{equation}
\texttt{request}(P_0).\texttt{fork}(k_1,s^{\mathrm{AR}}).\texttt{fork}(k_2,s^{\mathrm{diff}})
= [P_0,\;P_1^{\mathrm{AR}},\;P_2^{\mathrm{diff}}],
\label{eq:skeleton}
\end{equation}
where $s^{\mathrm{AR}}$ and $s^{\mathrm{diff}}$ are the stage sampling parameters. For $B$ root prompts, the resulting $B k_1$ AR rows and $B k_1 k_2$ image rows are fixed before any token or latent is sampled.
Every rollout engine implements one transformation over such a skeleton:
\begin{equation}
F_{\theta,e}: \mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{skeleton}} \longrightarrow \mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{filled}},
\label{eq:engine}
\end{equation}
where $e$ identifies a train-side or external generation runtime.
$F$ keeps the request's layer sequence and identifiers, writes decoded values, conditions, and a replay segment into the shells it fills, and stamps its behavior version on the frontier layer it returns; when an engine or trainer fills several shells through child engines, it copies each child's behavior version onto the layer that child filled.
The base contract also exposes lifecycle and weight-publication operations, but it does not standardize the engine's internal serving API.

\paragraph{One trajectory spans autoregressive and diffusion stages.}
The filled skeleton records every stage of a multi-stage generation in the trajectory itself.
Within a pipeline that fills several shells, a generated shell obtains its inputs from its ancestry: \texttt{conditioning\_at} walks each row's parent chain and collects the role-tagged primitives along it.
In the HunyuanImage-3.0 and BAGEL text-to-text-and-image modes, one pipeline fills the AR shell from the root prompt; the image shell's lineage then surfaces both the original prompt and the generated reasoning as its conditioning, without a side table that pairs each image with the text that produced it.
Each stage's replay state lands in its own layer---a \texttt{TextSegment} from an AR stage and a \texttt{LatentSegment} from a diffusion stage---so the filled tree carries what credit assignment and replay need for both stages (Section~\ref{sec:fold}).
An AR stage and a diffusion stage are thus two typed layers of one prompt-rooted tree rather than two batches joined after the fact.
The representation carries this cross-stage dataflow; stage order is declared by each workflow's skeleton.
The current trainers instantiate single-stage AR, single-stage diffusion, input--AR--diffusion, and multi-turn tool trajectories from the same representation.

\paragraph{Engine forms.}
The current source contains three direct forms of Equation~\ref{eq:engine} and one coordinator that composes it.
The train-side adapter invokes the model pipeline directly under evaluation mode, filling every generated shell of the request.
Dedicated serving adapters for SGLang, SGLang-Diffusion, vLLM, vLLM-Omni, and FastVideo translate typed trajectory inputs to their native requests and rebuild typed responses.
A composed engine serially fills the AR and diffusion shells of Equation~\ref{eq:skeleton} with two child engines.
An agentic harness composes Equation~\ref{eq:engine} over turns: it forks a policy turn, has a named engine fill it, and appends the environment observation before the next turn, returning a longer layer sequence.
The claim is therefore about a common boundary, not one universal generation kernel.

\subsection{Training folds the tree into per-stage updates}
\label{sec:fold}

Training traverses the filled tree in the opposite direction.
The reward role scores the frontier, which in the cross-stage layouts above is the image layer.
\texttt{propagate\_rewards} then reduces each scored layer onto its unscored parent layer by mean, maximum, or sum, so an upstream AR layer receives feedback from the images it conditioned.
Credit is computed per trainable layer: \texttt{compute\_advantages} labels each row by its ancestor at a chosen depth---the immediate parent by default, or the root prompt when requested---and, by default, normalizes rewards within each group.
For the image layer of Equation~\ref{eq:skeleton}, the two choices compare images under one rewrite or all images under one original prompt; selecting between them is a lineage query rather than a reconstructed join (Section~\ref{sec:rq3}).
Reduction and grouping consume siblings as contiguous blocks, the well-formedness property stated above.
Advantages are attached on the driver before the batch is scattered, so training shards receive precomputed credit rather than re-deriving groups.

Each trainable layer then becomes a training track bound to a train stack and a stage algorithm.
The trainer selects the layer by its sampling-parameter type and skips frozen layers.
In the composed prompt-enhancement workflow, the AR and diffusion models have separate stacks and optimizer steps; freezing the rewrite model, as E4 does, trains only the diffusion track.
When both layers belong to one backbone, as in HunyuanImage-3.0, a unified stack back-propagates the AR and image losses through one shared optimizer step.

\subsection{Component interfaces for stage replay and optimization}
\label{sec:algorithm-boundary}

Once credit is attached, each training track has three interfaces.

\paragraph{Trajectory layer.}
The layer supplies $(C_i, Z_i, A_i)$ for stage $i$: model-facing conditions $C_i$, the replay segment $Z_i$ (a \texttt{TextSegment} or a \texttt{LatentSegment}), and precomputed advantages $A_i$.

\paragraph{Stage algorithm.}
The algorithm defines the objective
\begin{equation}
(C,Z,A;\theta) \longmapsto \mathcal{L}\quad\text{and backward},
\label{eq:algorithm}
\end{equation}
over one shard's conditions, replay segment, and advantages.
The loss depends only on $(C,Z,A)$.
An optional per-shard preparation hook may read the full layer \parttype{}, for example per-row metadata or critic values, and an algorithm that needs reference or exponential moving average (EMA) weights holds an explicit backend handle.

\paragraph{Train stack.}
The stack plans micro-batches, can freeze an old-policy replay anchor at the training micro-batch geometry before repeated updates, accumulates and clips gradients, normalizes global losses, and steps the optimizer.

Under this split, AR objectives replay token log probabilities from \texttt{TextSegment}s, and diffusion objectives replay selected denoising transitions from \texttt{LatentSegment}s, comparing them with rollout statistics, a pre-update replay, or a reference model.
Supervised and teacher-distillation objectives do not use $A$; the forward-process DiffusionNFT objective consumes group advantages through the same signature.

Because models and algorithms meet only at this data boundary, they compose without pairwise adaptation.
A model is integrated once, as a bundle whose stages fill and replay typed segments; an algorithm is written once, as a loss over a stage's replay of those segments.
A shared algorithm can then be paired with any model whose stages produce and replay the segment fields it consumes---AR objectives with \texttt{TextSegment} producers and diffusion objectives with \texttt{LatentSegment} producers---so supporting $N_{\mathrm{mod}}$ models and $N_{\mathrm{alg}}$ shared algorithms requires $N_{\mathrm{mod}}+N_{\mathrm{alg}}$ integrations rather than $N_{\mathrm{mod}}\times N_{\mathrm{alg}}$; a model that needs a specialized objective adds it as a subclass without changing the others.
Variants that keep the Flow-GRPO loss~\citep{liu2025flowgrpo} (implemented by the \texttt{FlowGRPO} algorithm class) and change only the SDE transition or the step schedule, such as DanceGRPO and MixGRPO, are recipes rather than new algorithm classes.

This division is deliberately not advertised as algorithmic novelty.
GRPO follows the group-relative formulation introduced for language reasoning~\citep{shao2024deepseekmath}; PPO follows proximal policy optimization~\citep{schulman2017ppo}; diffusion objectives build on prior diffusion-RL methods~\citep{black2023ddpo,liu2025flowgrpo,xue2025dancegrpo,zheng2025diffusionnft}.
The systems question is whether these objectives can consume the same trajectory and execution contracts without hiding their different sufficient statistics.

\subsection{Logical roles, device slabs, and worker slots}

A \texttt{DevicePool} creates one Ray worker per GPU initially and reserves one strict-packed placement group per node.
Each worker occupies a slot on its GPU: slot~0 exists from startup, further slots are created on demand up to a per-GPU limit, and every slot reserves an equal fraction of that GPU.
A top-level placement scope claims a fraction of the whole device pool from the still-unclaimed devices; a nested scope takes a fraction of its parent's devices.
Roles created inside a scope either share its worker slot---enabling local sibling access and memory time-sharing---or receive isolated slots on the same devices (isolated slots require the per-GPU store transport).
Separate top-level scopes form separate training, rollout, or reward slabs.

A role is a regular Python object derived from \texttt{Remote}.
The handle constructs one role replica on each selected worker and derives data-, tensor-, pipeline-, sequence-, and expert-parallel rank metadata from the role configuration.
Method decorators declare a dispatch mode (broadcast, scatter, or DP scatter) and which ranks execute; the dispatch mode determines how results are collected.
The trainer is consequently a single-controller program, while each method may invoke multi-controller distributed training or serving inside a role.
This resembles HybridFlow's separation between orchestration and distributed computation~\citep{sheng2024hybridflow}; UniRL's paper claim centers instead on carrying the same typed lineage through heterogeneous generative stages.

\subsection{Tensor references decouple structure from movement}

In the single-controller design, every role method is a remote call issued by the driver, and its result returns to the driver before being dispatched to the next role.
If trajectories carried dense tensors across these hops, latent snapshots, token log probabilities, and decoded media would pass through the driver on every call, and the driver would have to materialize them merely to split, select, or scatter complete root groups.
UniRL therefore routes control through the driver but keeps tensor data on the workers.
When a role method returns, its worker stores every dense tensor leaf in a local transport and \emph{dehydrates} it into a \texttt{TensorRef}: an ordered list of half-open row spans over opaque handles, plus shape, dtype, and device metadata.
The driver receives only this dehydrated trajectory; selection and contiguous slicing construct new span views without moving tensor data.
Before the next call, the transport localizes each reference for its destination worker, leaving resident spans in place and transferring the others worker-to-worker.
The destination worker then \emph{hydrates} all referenced spans in one batch and writes the tensors back into the nested object in place before invoking the method; spans whose trailing widths differ are zero-padded to a common width.
Control decisions on the driver need only small fields such as rewards, which it hydrates on demand.

The transport interface has a deliberately small contract: store tensors, resolve handles, identify references, and optionally localize references for destination workers.
The audited source provides (i) a colocated per-worker store, (ii) a per-GPU store with IPC and NCCL transfers, and (iii) a TransferQueue path backed by simple storage or Mooncake-compatible storage.
Mooncake itself was proposed for disaggregated serving~\citep{qin2024mooncake}; here its storage interface is a transport option rather than a claimed UniRL invention.

The driver can therefore manipulate trajectory structure and metadata without materializing all GPU tensors.
Whether this reduces memory and transfer cost in practice is RQ3, not an assumption.

\subsection{Residency lifecycles and weight publication}
\label{sec:lifecycle}

Training and generation often hold different copies or formats of a policy.
We distinguish two version counters: the \emph{optimizer version} $v_t$, which counts committed optimizer updates on the training stack, and the \emph{behavior version} $v_b$, which the rollout engine stamps onto each generated frontier layer.
In every deployment, a due weight publication completes before the generation it serves; asynchronous trainers update $v_b$ to $v_t$ through the rollout manager upon publication (Section~\ref{sec:async}), the agentic trainer sets $v_b = v_t$ directly at each publication, and the remaining synchronous cases are described below.
How weight publication interleaves with memory residency depends on whether the two roles share devices.

\paragraph{Colocated slabs.}
When training and rollout time-share one GPU set, the engine is put to sleep---releasing its weight and cache memory---between generations and woken before the next one, and training state may be offloaded to host memory while the engine is awake.
Every colocated order keeps two invariants: a due publication completes before generation, and memory is released before the role that needs it is brought up, so the engine sleeps before offloaded training state is restored.
The default order wakes the engine, publishes weights if due, offloads training state if enabled, and generates; afterwards it sleeps the engine and restores the training state.
The AR and agentic trainers follow this order, and the AR trainer adapts it to engine capabilities: an engine that can restore its weight memory separately from its cache receives published weights before allocating the rest, and an engine pinned to one GPU of the slab receives weights that are extracted before training state is offloaded and published after the engine wakes (Appendix~\ref{app:residency}).
The diffusion trainers express residency as one resident-or-offloaded choice per role (training, rollout, reward): they offload every non-resident role before waking the engine, publish weights into the awake engine, sleep it after generation unless the rollout role is resident, and restore an offloaded trainer only before its optimizer step.
The prompt-enhancement trainer follows the default order, and the unified-model trainer with separate AR and diffusion engines uses the same extract-then-publish order.
A train-side engine reads live model objects, so training state is never offloaded for it.

\paragraph{Separate slabs.}
When training and rollout occupy separate GPU slabs, generation needs no memory handoff:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item at startup, connect the training side to the rollout workers through a one-time handshake;
  \item at each publication point, publish parameters across slabs, under the admission discipline of Section~\ref{sec:async} for asynchronous trainers;
  \item generate and stamp the frontier layer with the engine's current behavior version $v_b$.
\end{enumerate}
Generation never offloads training state to make room for the engine.
The synchronous diffusion trainer sleeps the engine between rollouts unless the rollout role is resident and, when the training role is not resident, offloads training state while a reward role shares the training slab.
The asynchronous trainers keep the engine awake; the asynchronous diffusion trainer also requires a resident reward role.

Weight-publication implementations cover in-process and cross-slab Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) adapter publication as well as full-weight publication via serialized tensors, CUDA IPC (ZMQ or vLLM's native weight-transfer engine), SGLang checkpoint-engine IPC, NCCL broadcast, and a checkpoint-file handoff.
The base engine can also receive per-track updates for composed models.

The stamped behavior version $v_b$ tracks optimizer-version provenance on the asynchronous and agentic paths, which set $v_b = v_t$ at each publication.
The synchronous trainers do not advance $v_b$, so their frontiers keep version zero, except when publishing through vLLM's native weight-transfer engine, which increments $v_b$ on each received publication.

The source rejects combinations whose lifecycle has not been validated, such as a train-side rollout on a physically separate slab or selected EMA/offload combinations.
This fail-closed policy is important because a partially awakened engine or silently stale adapter can produce plausible but invalid training data.
Section~\ref{sec:evaluation} therefore includes replay-consistency and failure-injection checks alongside throughput.

\subsection{Synchronous execution}

The synchronous AR trainer performs rollout, reward, credit assignment, replay/update, optional evaluation, and checkpointing in order.
The diffusion trainer can accumulate multiple rollouts into one optimizer window and can place reward on the train slab or a third slab, or score micro-batches inside the colocated rollout window so that reward overlaps generation.
Topology is a placement choice made through the same scopes for every modality.
The synchronous AR trainer builds one colocated slab, optionally pinning the engine to one of its GPUs, and the synchronous diffusion trainer additionally builds separate training and rollout slabs.
Separate-slab AR without overlap is the asynchronous trainer with one prompt batch in flight, publication after every batch, and zero allowed lag (Section~\ref{sec:async}): it admits the next batch only after publishing the weights produced from the previous one, so generation and training strictly alternate.
In every case, topology changes resource ownership, residency, and the publication path, but not the trajectory boundary: the same \sample{} passes from rollout through reward and credit to the train stack.
This makes a controlled topology experiment possible: hold model, prompts, group size, reward, number of replayed steps or tokens, and optimizer updates constant while changing only engine or placement.
The publication path and training DP width change with the placement and are reported as part of it.

\subsection{Bounded-staleness execution}
\label{sec:async}

Asynchrony changes correctness conditions, not just control flow.
UniRL's asynchronous AR and diffusion trainers run on separate training and rollout slabs and share one manager.
The driver submits whole prompt batches only while they fit the in-flight capacity, the freshness horizon implied by the staleness allowance, and the batches remaining before the next evaluation, checkpoint, or final boundary.
A background dispatcher then fills every engine slot up to a configured per-worker capacity.
Completed trajectories are grouped by root; only complete groups enter the ready buffer.
Every generated layer must carry a behavior version $v_b$.

Let $v_t$ be the current optimizer version, which counts committed optimizer steps, and $u$ the configured number of optimizer updates per consumed rollout batch.
The manager exposes lag in update units,
\begin{equation}
\ell = v_t-v_b \leq u b,
\end{equation}
where $b$ is the staleness allowance in rollout-batch units (configurable for AR, fixed to the publication interval minus one for diffusion).
The filter measures each trajectory by its oldest generated layer and discards whole root groups beyond that budget, including incomplete roots held across a publication.
Because a skipped non-finite step does not advance $v_t$, admitted work can be more than $b$ rollout batches old.
Before publishing optimizer version $v_t$, the manager pauses admission, drains or suspends work, completes any started or batch-aligning prefix, checks that no publication splits a training batch across behavior versions, publishes weights, updates the engine's behavior version to $v_t$, and then resubmits eligible pristine carry.
Evaluation and checkpointing require a fully empty manager.

This discipline differs from claiming fully asynchronous optimization.
Weight publication remains a barrier.
The asynchronous diffusion trainer admits one prompt batch at a time and requires each training batch to come from a single generation request, so its next generation overlaps only the optimizer step; the AR trainer may keep several batches in flight and refills before reward scoring.
The evaluation must report the actual lag histogram, dropped work, and barrier time; a nominal ``async'' label is insufficient.

\section{Instantiations of the Common Contracts}
\label{sec:instantiations}

The following paths show which information crosses the common boundary and which computations remain specialized.
They illustrate the contracts at the design level, as implemented in the audited source, rather than inventory the system's capabilities.
Section~\ref{sec:evaluation} exercises only the group-relative AR objectives, Flow-GRPO, and the frozen-rewrite cross-stage path; the GAE, DiffusionNFT, rewrite-training, and agentic paths described below have stock recipes but no reported training runs.

\subsection{Autoregressive reasoning}

An input layer carries prompt text and metadata.
Forking by group size creates generated shells with sibling IDs.
An AR engine fills packed token segments and decoded text, and a reward role attaches scalar or component rewards.
Group-normalized credit is computed on the driver before the batch is scattered; token-level generalized advantage estimation (GAE) is instead computed per trajectory on the training workers, after the critic anchor is frozen.
The train stack replays the same segment and applies the selected objective.
Optional token-length balancing permutes individual generated rows of the training layer before the train stack scatters them, after driver-side group credit but before per-trajectory GAE, which does not depend on row order.
It keeps each row's identifier and any attached advantage but abandons sibling contiguity, so no grouping operation may follow it; the E3-AR UniRL recipe enables it.

\subsection{Diffusion and flow generation}

A diffusion request forks prompts and records sampling parameters, selected SDE indices, and an initial-noise recipe; the hosting engine pins the sigma schedule onto the shell before generation.
The engine returns decoded media plus a latent segment whose sparse trajectory fields are sufficient for the selected update.
Flow-GRPO-style objectives replay recorded steps and compare new transition log probabilities against a frozen rollout or replay anchor~\citep{liu2025flowgrpo}.
Forward-process objectives such as DiffusionNFT use clean generated samples rather than the same reverse-trajectory likelihood path~\citep{zheng2025diffusionnft}.
The common train stack handles micro-batching and optimizer state; the algorithm remains responsible for these incompatible estimators.

\subsection{Cross-stage and environment-interacting trajectories}

In a prompt-enhancement path, one root produces one or more AR rewrites, and every rewrite produces one or more images (Equation~\ref{eq:skeleton}).
The image reward is attached to the leaf; when the rewrite stage trains, parent-major reduction supplies its feedback.
The primary E4 case freezes that stage and uses ancestry to select the diffusion advantage groups.
The prompt-enhancement workflow needs this ancestry to survive rollout scheduling and sharding; flattening it into unrelated rows would require rebuilding that correspondence out of band.
In a tool-interacting path, each trajectory is a single-branch chain: the policy emits an action-like text layer, the environment appends an observation layer, and the process repeats until termination or suspension.
The agentic trainer keeps one \sample{} per trajectory, forms a group from repeated copies of the same root rather than forked siblings, computes group advantages over those trajectories, and trains on their flattened generated turns, each inheriting its trajectory's advantage, so lineage there conditions each turn within a trajectory rather than defining groups or shards.

E4 checks these groups against an explicit ID-based reference on synthetic fixtures and, for group membership and advantages, on recorded rollouts, and then studies the two grouping objectives in training.
Before turning to the main E0--E6 evaluation program in Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}, Sections~\ref{sec:reward} and~\ref{sec:fidelity} present two targeted case studies that examine how the reward-serving and rollout-engine contracts behave on instruction-guided image editing.

\paragraph{Diffusion workload.}
E2 fixes SD3.5-Medium~\citep{sd35medium2024} and the UniRL Flow-GRPO setting used by E3, i.e.\ the stock \codepath{sd3\_vllmomni.yaml} recipe with the alignment overrides in \codepath{run\_unirl\_sd35\_aligned.sh}: 48 prompts with 16 images (768 samples) per rollout, $384\times384$ training resolution, ten denoising steps, three SDE steps in the first half, $\eta=0.8$, guidance 1, and distinct initial noise.
PickScore~\citep{kirstain2023pickapic} supplies the training reward; advantages subtract each prompt-group mean and divide by the batch-wide reward standard deviation.
LoRA rank/alpha is 32/64 on eight matched attention projections; the learner uses two updates, micro-batch size eight, learning rate and weight decay $10^{-4}$, clip $10^{-5}$, and weight publication every rollout.
We prespecify 300 rollouts on one eight-GPU node with seed IDs 11, 22, and 33.
The same budget and seed IDs apply to the pinned VeRL-Omni reference run (E2-R); checkpoint export and estimator parity are audited before admission.
Because the stock configurations differ in SDE transition strategy---UniRL uses \texttt{FlowSDEStrategy} whereas VeRL-Omni uses Coefficients-Preserving Sampling (CPS)---as well as step selection and normalization/anchor semantics, E2 and E2-R currently define a related-recipe comparison. At least one full-budget E1 or E2 reference must pass mathematical and work parity before the paper claims reference-implementation reproduction.
Frozen base and every 50-rollout checkpoint use a shared diffusers evaluation pipeline. The primary image endpoint is PartiPrompts HPSv3~\citep{ma2025hpsv3}; ImageReward~\citep{xu2023imagereward} and compositional scores provide complementary checks.
These three are held-fixed automatic judges distinct from the optimized PickScore model, not independent human studies; Table~\ref{tab:rq1} also reports held-out PickScore on the PartiPrompts set, explicitly labeled as the training reward, to record movement on the optimized objective itself.

\paragraph{Image evaluation identity.}

The prespecified image endpoints use $512\times512$, 40 steps, guidance 1, and an evaluation seed of 42 shared across training seeds and checkpoints.
The E2 preference rows were generated with 16 images for each of 1,632 prompts, the sample count the diversity guard requires, rather than the original one image per prompt.
Image seeds are indexed by prompt and sample and do not depend on the sample count, so the one-image endpoint is the first-sample subset of these rows; that subset has not been scored separately.
The synthetic endpoint additionally fixes its explicit linear sigma grid, prompt-hashed seeds, and 256-token text-encoder limit; the preference endpoint fixes the pinned pipeline schedule and index-based seeds.
Dataset sizes, revisions, overlap counts, and scorer hashes remain open manifest fields.

The diversity guard is within-prompt mean pairwise LPIPS~\citep{zhang2018lpips} (AlexNet features), computed over 16 images per PartiPrompts prompt at the same evaluation setting, with identical image seeds for base and checkpoint.
We average all 120 pair distances per prompt, then report the relative change in the mean across prompts and a paired prompt-bootstrap interval.
A greater than 10\% relative decrease is a study-specific investigation trigger; it is not a literature-established quality threshold. A near-zero base makes this relative criterion undefined and requires the absolute change to be reported.
This guard diagnoses reduced diversity and is not itself a perceptual-quality metric.

\paragraph{AR systems result.}
Across the 499 measured rollouts, UniRL's mean raw end-to-end iteration time is 2.6\% higher than veRL's ($143.519$\,s vs.\ $139.894$\,s), and its policy generates 11.0\% longer responses on average ($4{,}197.6$ vs.\ $3{,}781.8$ tokens) with a higher fraction of completions reaching the 8,192-token cap ($0.307$ vs.\ $0.203$ truncation rate).
The median raw end-to-end iteration time is 147.7\,s for UniRL and 150.1\,s for veRL, and the 90th percentiles are 172.6\,s and 169.6\,s.
Longer responses and more frequent 8,192-token truncation increase per-step token volume (lengthening raw iteration time) but also raise output-token throughput (tok/s) by amortizing per-step and prefill overhead over longer decode sequences, so response length and truncation act as a workload confounder rather than a one-sided handicap.
When normalized by total generated output tokens, UniRL records 8.2\% higher end-to-end output-token throughput ($14{,}975$ vs.\ $13{,}841$\,tok/s) and 17.7\% higher generation output-token throughput ($28{,}257$ vs.\ $24{,}018$\,tok/s).
In raw phase time per step, UniRL is faster in generation by 4.56\,s (5.7\% lower) but slower in training by 5.45\,s (9.8\% higher), alongside separate reward ($2.27$\,s) and weight-publication ($3.08$\,s) stages; only on a per-output-token basis is UniRL's generation advantage (+17.7\% tok/s) larger than the training difference (where output tokens processed per training second are within 1.1\%: $35{,}119$ vs.\ $34{,}735$\,tok/s).
The raw end-to-end ordering reverses near rollout 300 as the two sampled response-length distributions cross, while UniRL's token-normalized end-to-end and generation throughput remain higher in all five 100-rollout bands.
Phase values use each framework's native instrumentation and are diagnostic; end-to-end time has the clearest common boundary.
Two estimator-level differences remain that configuration cannot remove: UniRL divides advantages by the population standard deviation and veRL by the sample standard deviation, making veRL's advantages uniformly $\sqrt{7/8}=0.935\times$ smaller at eight samples per prompt, and the two advantage epsilons differ by $10^{-8}$ versus $10^{-6}$; both are quantified against their closed forms in the bundle's audit.

\subsection{RQ3: What does the trajectory abstraction cost and enable?}
\label{sec:rq3}

RQ3 first tests the semantic capability that motivates the IR (E4, comprising synthetic equality verification E4a and three-seed training comparison E4b), and then measures its representation and topology costs (E5, comprising the paired representation microbenchmark E5-R and the deployment-topology comparison E5-T).
E4 uses the executable frozen-Qwen3-0.6B rewrite $\rightarrow$ SD3.5-Medium path: eight original prompts, four rewrites per prompt, and eight images per rewrite, or 256 image descendants per rollout; only diffusion trains.
This is a separate recipe from E2: $512\times512$, ten steps, one sampled SDE index among 0--8, $\eta=0.7$, LoRA 16/32, learning rate $3\times10^{-4}$, weight decay zero, clip $10^{-4}$, replay anchor, two updates, and micro-batch size one.
The primary condition (\texttt{diffusion\_group\_scope=prompt}) computes group-relative advantages across all 32 descendants of one original prompt, while \texttt{diffusion\_group\_scope=rewrite} (the trainer default) groups the eight images under each rewrite.
The two stock prompt-enhancement recipes are not a matched pair: the prompt-group recipe uses eight images per rewrite and a 512-token rewrite limit, whereas the rewrite-scope recipe uses four and 256. Both arms must therefore use the prompt-group recipe with only the grouping key changed, as confirmed by their resolved configurations; both stock recipes also enable \texttt{shuffle\_updates} at the audited release.
Both conditions must serialize redacted root/layer/parent IDs, shapes, reward/advantage, replay indices, and group labels; the synchronous prompt-enhancement trainer does not advance behavior versions, so they carry no behavior-version provenance here.
Invalid lineage remains an E0 fail-fast test rather than an intentionally corrupted quality baseline.

For image $x_{p,j,k}$, the two group keys are $g_{\rm root}=p$ and $g_{\rm rewrite}=(p,j)$.
The intended semantic test (E4a) requires an ID-joined flat-row reference to reproduce UniRL's group memberships, advantages, and learner inputs on identical recorded rewards and replay tensors, including after root-shard round trips.
The 300-rollout, three-seed training comparison (E4b) changes the normalization population (32 versus eight images); any quality difference would therefore concern the grouping objective, not isolate the IR representation's effect.

Passing that recorded-trajectory control would establish the grouping semantics on real rollout data; it would not by itself prove better task quality.
The current PickScore request is conditioned by the rewritten text (the default \texttt{prompt\_source: generation}; scoring against the root prompt with \texttt{prompt\_source: original} is available but was not used in E4), so a claim about preserving the user's original intent additionally requires lineage-keyed evaluation of every image against its root prompt.
We therefore report both rewrite-conditioned training reward and frozen HPSv3/ImageReward evaluations joined to each root prompt.
Evaluation generates held-out images at $512\times512$, 40 steps, and guidance 1 with the base and every 50-rollout diffusion checkpoint, using one fixed manifest of four frozen rewrites per root and eight image seeds per rewrite for both conditions.
Worker/adapter RNG initialization, deterministic train-side AR sampling, real trace dumping, and this held-out generation/evaluation path are E0 prerequisites for a multi-seed E4 result.
If the two correct groupings tie, the experiment still establishes semantic expressiveness but not a quality benefit.

\subsection{RQ4: Exploratory bounded-staleness observations}
\label{sec:rq4}

The prespecified E6 study uses the E1 Qwen3-4B-Base GRPO workload on DAPO-Math-17k across 32 GPUs (64 prompts $\times$ eight samples per rollout, four optimizer updates per rollout), separating the device-allocation contrast---colocated synchronous execution on 32 GPUs (\texttt{S0}) versus disaggregated 16-training/16-rollout execution with one batch in flight, publication every batch, and zero allowed policy lag (\texttt{D0})---from the onset of generation--training overlap (\texttt{D0} versus \texttt{A1}, with two batches in flight, publication every batch, and max lag $b=1$), followed by staleness-allowance and publication-interval points (\texttt{A2}: in-flight 2, interval 1, max lag 2; \texttt{A3}: in-flight 2, interval 2, max lag 2; \texttt{A4}: in-flight 2, interval 2, max lag 4).
The formal grid sweep was canceled on 2026-09-21 after preliminary runs; only single-seed capacity measurements (with evaluation disabled) and a separate quality-target pilot on \texttt{D0} were completed.
In the single-seed capacity runs, reported per-rollout durations were $82.34$\,s for \texttt{S0}, $103.07$\,s for zero-overlap disaggregated \texttt{D0}, and $56.48$\,s (\texttt{A1}), $55.19$\,s (\texttt{A2}), $33.47$\,s (\texttt{A3}), and $39.54$\,s (\texttt{A4}, pooled over two replicates) once overlap was enabled.
Because those preliminary timings were collected at one seed with evaluation disabled---and before aligning five stock learner configuration differences between the synchronous and asynchronous recipes---they serve only as planning observations rather than a controlled speedup or equal-quality comparison.

\paragraph{The zero-lag quality-target pilot did not pass.}
Before running the overlapped grid for quality, the protocol required registering a target MATH-500 threshold from the disaggregated zero-overlap baseline \texttt{D0}.
In that pilot, \texttt{D0}'s reported MATH-500 avg@4 decreased from \ESixQualityEarly{} at rollout 150 to \ESixQualityLate{} at rollout 300, contrary to the prespecified requirement that the later checkpoint improve, and training reward declined over the same window.
No quality threshold was registered and no time-to-quality result is reported.
Because \texttt{D0} enforces one batch in flight, weight publication after every batch, and zero allowed lag ($\ell = 0$, so generation and training strictly alternate without staleness; Section~\ref{sec:execution}), its degradation reflects the training horizon and learner dynamics on the zero-lag baseline rather than policy staleness.
Because no $\ell > 0$ or overlapped configuration was evaluated for quality before cancellation, E6 provides no empirical evidence on how bounded staleness affects model quality.
Two checkpoints are also insufficient to locate a precise turning point.

\paragraph{Evaluation variability and requirements for a restart.}
Repeated evaluation of a single checkpoint exhibited noticeable prompt-level avg@4 variance despite a small aggregate difference.
That observation is specific to the sampled checkpoint and decoding protocol; it is not a universal noise floor or a substitute for training-seed replication.
A renewed study would first audit scorer behavior, request-level sampling, and repeat-evaluation variability; match the synchronous/asynchronous estimator and learner settings; and record complete driver time and buffer admission, rejection, discard, retry, and completion accounting.
The trainer's internal \texttt{perf/step\_time\_s} metric alone omits outer-loop weight publication, evaluation, and checkpoint work.
Only after establishing a suitable learning budget and a separately piloted target should a replicated formal grid support a lag--quality--time conclusion.

\subsection{Fairness and statistical protocol}

Effective work is matched before wall-clock time: prompts, samples per prompt, decoded resolution or maximum tokens, rollout steps, replayed steps/tokens, micro-batching semantics, and optimizer updates.
Primary full-system metrics count all allocated training, rollout, and reward resources on both sides. Reward placement and concurrency must be disclosed; a reward-excluded number is supplementary and carries a separate service-cost report.
We prespecify warm-up removal, use at least 20 steady-state iterations for throughput summaries, and report the full distribution rather than a single minimum.
Training summaries use the seed as the statistical unit. The E1, E2, and E4 tables and figures show every seed, with seed-level 95\% $t$ intervals where a mean is reported; with three seeds these intervals have two degrees of freedom and are wide by construction. Bootstrap intervals over prompts or images must be labeled separately and cannot replace seed uncertainty.
The complete systems release must show all process repetitions and the post-warm-up per-rollout distribution; uncertainty of a speed ratio must use repetitions, not correlated rollouts, as its independent units.
E3-AR releases its 499-rollout post-warm-up distribution, but has only one process-level run per framework; the older report-derived E3 summaries provide neither the complete per-rollout distribution nor independently revalidated repetitions.
The final checkpoint and named primary endpoint are fixed in advance; the auxiliary metrics diagnose failure modes and are not alternative endpoints chosen after seeing results.
Any future E6 time-to-quality study must use MATH-500 and a target prespecified from a separate pilot before the formal grid, bracket first observed crossings by evaluation times, and censor runs that never cross. The canceled study produced no such result.
Elapsed time spans initialization, training, publication, checkpointing, and evaluation; steady-state throughput is reported separately.
All published cells require complete scoring or an explicit missingness analysis; silently averaging only successful evaluator responses is inadmissible.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{LLM post-training systems.}
HybridFlow/veRL combines single- and multi-controller execution and introduces hierarchical APIs for mapping RLHF dataflows to devices~\citep{sheng2024hybridflow}.
OpenRLHF uses Ray, vLLM, and DeepSpeed to schedule the models in an RLHF workflow~\citep{hu2024openrlhf}.
ROLL emphasizes a single-controller abstraction, parallel workers, sample lifecycle scheduling, and flexible device mapping~\citep{wang2025roll}.
NeMo-RL provides scalable training and generation backends and multimodal model support~\citep{nemorl2025}.
Group-relative recipes in these systems~\citep{sheng2024hybridflow,hu2024openrlhf,wang2025roll,nemorl2025} typically record one level of sampling structure---responses grouped under their prompt by a per-prompt identifier or by contiguous sample order---whereas UniRL generalizes that group key to multi-level ancestry encoded in sample identifiers.
These systems already drive distinct generation and training backends from one control loop and colocate or separate those backends as a placement choice; UniRL keeps that split and changes the object the loop schedules.
Scheduling and DP sharding move complete root groups rather than rows, and each generated layer carries the replay segment for its own AR or diffusion stage (Section~\ref{sec:trajectory}).
A flat-row batch can recover the same group memberships by joining on retained parent keys (Section~\ref{sec:problem}); UniRL differs in that sharding, credit assignment, and cross-stage conditioning consume that ancestry in place, so changing the execution path does not require rebuilding the join.
RQ3 specifies the corresponding control: an ID-joined reference on recorded rewards and replay tensors (Section~\ref{sec:rq3}).
To date its agreement check covers group membership and advantages on recorded rollouts and replay tensors only on synthetic fixtures, and the paired cost of carrying the structure (E5-R) is unmeasured; the single-process CPU regression is not that measurement.

\paragraph{Asynchronous post-training.}
AReaL fully decouples generation and training and controls staleness to improve language-reasoning throughput~\citep{fu2025areal}.
Relax uses service-level separation, a TransferQueue data bus, and configurable staleness for omni-modal post-training~\citep{zhang2026relax}.
slime supports persistent asynchronous rollout generation around a training/rollout/data-buffer loop~\citep{slime2025}.
These systems make asynchrony an execution design space.
UniRL takes a conservative point within it: weight publication remains a barrier, and lag is bounded in optimizer-update units (Section~\ref{sec:async}).
Each generated layer carries its behavior version in the trajectory IR, while admission, lag filtering, and weight publication stay in one manager shared by the AR and diffusion trainers.
The filter keeps or drops whole root groups so that group-relative baselines stay complete, and no training batch spans behavior versions; this is stricter than AReaL, whose decoupled objective admits trajectories generated under several behavior versions~\citep{fu2025areal}.
RQ4 reports only single-seed capacity measurements and a quality-target pilot that did not pass; it supports no throughput or time-to-quality comparison with these systems (Section~\ref{sec:rq4}).

\paragraph{Multimodal and diffusion post-training systems.}
VeRL-Omni extends veRL toward diffusion, unified, and omni-modality models and integrates vLLM-Omni rollout~\citep{verlomni2026}.
Relax is explicitly omni-native~\citep{zhang2026relax}.
Modality coverage therefore does not distinguish UniRL from these systems.
The distinction is how ancestry and typed replay segments survive execution: an AR stage and a diffusion stage are two layers of one \sample{} (Section~\ref{sec:trajectory}), and tensor references move their dense state without changing that lineage (Section~\ref{sec:execution}).
Models and algorithms meet only at the per-stage conditions, replay segment, and advantages that an algorithm consumes, so $N_{\mathrm{mod}}$ models and $N_{\mathrm{alg}}$ shared algorithms require $N_{\mathrm{mod}}+N_{\mathrm{alg}}$ rather than $N_{\mathrm{mod}}\times N_{\mathrm{alg}}$ integrations (Section~\ref{sec:algorithm-boundary}).
E4 runs one frozen AR rewrite stage into a trained diffusion stage (Section~\ref{sec:rq3}).
Its lineage check covers group membership and advantages on recorded rollouts and replay tensors only on synthetic fixtures, and its quality comparison, rerun with a corrected rewrite template, is inconclusive at three seed pairs; the pending module inventory would list what that path reuses rather than measuring human integration effort.

\paragraph{Diffusion RL algorithms.}
DDPO casts denoising as a multi-step decision process~\citep{black2023ddpo}.
Flow-GRPO converts flow sampling to an SDE and applies group-relative optimization~\citep{liu2025flowgrpo}; DanceGRPO generalizes related ideas across visual generation tasks~\citep{xue2025dancegrpo}; DiffusionNFT instead optimizes a forward-process objective from reward-ranked generations~\citep{zheng2025diffusionnft}.
UniRL consumes Flow-GRPO-style and DiffusionNFT objectives through its algorithm interface and claims neither objective (Section~\ref{sec:instantiations}).
What the interface contributes is that their incompatible sufficient statistics stay explicit: Flow-GRPO-style objectives replay recorded SDE transitions, DiffusionNFT trains on clean generated samples, and both reuse the same train stack for micro-batching and optimizer state.

\section{Limitations and Open Evidence}
\label{sec:limitations}

\paragraph{Integration cost is per architecture.}
Adding an architecture requires one bundle with its stages, conditions, and rollout adapters; this cost is paid once per model rather than once per model--algorithm pair.
Architecture-specific details such as tokenizers, noise schedulers, latent layouts, and LoRA targets stay inside that bundle and its adapters rather than being abstracted by the trajectory type.
The ordered-layer representation targets trajectories that grow by forking and observation; arbitrary ragged forests are outside its current scope.

\paragraph{Topology is configured, not optimized.}
Placement scopes make resource choices explicit but do not search for the best split.
The best topology depends on model memory, reward cost, rollout variance, interconnect, and publication size.

\paragraph{Asynchronous execution is bounded and partially specialized.}
Weight publication is a barrier, and current AR and diffusion paths impose different admissible in-flight structure.
Fault isolation is limited by the shared Ray runtime and role lifecycle; the system does not yet claim service-level recovery comparable to dedicated service architectures.

\paragraph{Case-study scope.}
The reward-serving and rollout-fidelity case studies (Sections~\ref{sec:reward} and~\ref{sec:fidelity}) evaluate targeted mechanisms outside the E0--E6 program on a single BAGEL image-editing workload with small rollout counts per arm.
In Section~\ref{sec:reward-results}, the image-editing wall-clock durations are rescaled estimates that combine measured reward-side traces with the generation speedup from restored source conditioning; in Section~\ref{sec:reward-mps} and Section~\ref{sec:fidelity}, the MPS colocation runtime, token-layout check, per-group canvas, sibling packing, and cost-aware load balancer are evaluated from open UniRL pull requests (\#492, \#547--\#550) or an experimental branch rather than the v0.2.0 release commit.

\paragraph{The empirical case is incomplete.}
The GPU evidence supports qualified observations about diffusion scores and diversity, early autoregressive learning followed by degradation under the prespecified GRPO recipe and improvement under a DRPO recipe added later with one seed whose final answers mostly do not terminate, correct lineage grouping in a composed run, native-recipe iteration time, deployment sensitivity, and one recipe-matched AR throughput comparison.
The AR systems comparison releases per-rollout evidence but lacks independent process repetitions and a common external quality endpoint; it therefore does not establish run-to-run uncertainty, training-quality reproduction, or time-to-quality.
The combined evidence also does not establish mathematically aligned reference reproduction, improvement by the registered AR recipe, a quality advantage from lineage grouping, bounded IR overhead, scaling, or a time-to-quality benefit from asynchrony.
The E3, E5-T, and E6 values remain report-derived: their raw GPU records are unavailable, and they still need exact run manifests, consistent aggregate provenance, and independent regeneration.
The E0, E1, E2, and E4 values are generated from exported data, but their raw artifacts and run manifests are not yet released.
Lineage validation on recorded rollouts covers group membership and advantages, not replay tensors, and the corrected cross-stage comparison is inconclusive at three seed pairs.
The GRPO degradation is unexplained: no reference implementation was run, and the DRPO recipe that improves differs from GRPO's in five settings, so its three seeds do not isolate the objective.
The ordering of the two recipes at the final checkpoint also holds only as graded: it fails when one DRPO seed's answers at the token limit are counted wrong.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

UniRL treats post-training as a trajectory program whose semantic object survives changes in generative family and execution topology.
Lineage, typed modality values, replay segments, reward, credit, and behavior version live in one IR; rollout, reward, training, transport, and weight publication are logical roles mapped separately to resources.
Two case studies on instruction-guided image editing illustrate how these boundaries operate in practice: micro-batched generation--reward overlap hides remote judge latency when the judge has spare capacity, while structural token-layout checks and sibling request packing across the rollout-engine boundary recover conditioning fidelity and reduce generation time.
In the main evaluation program, the current evidence shows diffusion score improvements accompanied by reduced diversity, an autoregressive GRPO recipe that learns early and then degrades below its base model while a DRPO recipe added later improves on it in all three seeds as graded, a composed run in which both lineage-defined groupings are computed correctly and train, faster iteration of one native diffusion configuration, a substantial penalty for the measured separate deployment, and 8.2\% higher end-to-end output-token throughput for UniRL in one recipe-matched Qwen3-4B-Base GRPO run.
That AR systems result is a workload-specific throughput observation rather than a repeated-run or time-to-quality conclusion.
The full trajectory-centric claim remains open until replay-level lineage validation on recorded payloads and matched representation-cost measurements complement those observations, and bounded staleness remains an exploratory operating-region question with no demonstrated time-to-quality benefit in the canceled study.

\section{Required run artifact schema}
\label{app:artifacts}

Every result row must resolve to a run directory containing:
\begin{itemize}
  \item source and baseline commit hashes, submodule hashes, and dirty patches;
  \item the launch command and fully resolved configuration;
  \item model, reward, dataset, and tokenizer revisions or content hashes;
  \item GPU model/count, node/interconnect description, driver, CUDA, PyTorch, generation-engine, and training-backend versions;
  \item raw stdout/stderr, per-rollout structured timing, memory and utilization traces, checkpoint metadata, and evaluation outputs;
  \item seed, warm-up exclusion rule, failure/retry accounting, and statistical aggregation script.
\end{itemize}

For training runs, serialized trace samples must redact private data while retaining lineage IDs, shapes, dtypes, behavior versions, reward components, and segment index metadata.
For system baselines, an alignment manifest must explain every nonidentical setting and whether it changes effective work.

\section{Correctness status and remaining checks}
\label{app:correctness}

\paragraph{Executed locally.}
The preliminary RQ3 harness performs ten fail-fast checks on the audited source:
lineage-valid cross-stage construction; exact split/concat; tree-complete select and
slice; mean reward propagation plus group-normalized advantages; rejection of bad
lineage, modality keys, and primitive batch sizes; packed variable-length text
select/concat; and lazy tensor-reference selection, materialization, identity, and
bounds behavior. The structured outcomes and data-sensitive SHA-256 round-trip hash
are stored with the run artifacts.

\paragraph{Required before complete evidence admission.}
The following system-level checks are the evidence requirements, not a claim that every experiment has passed each check.
After each requirement, \emph{Status} summarizes what the 2026-10-08 bundle and the experiment records show.
E0's own test suite and configuration artifacts are not in this repository and were not revalidated here.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Rollout--replay parity:} before the first update, compare engine-emitted and train-side replay log probabilities or transition statistics under stated numerical tolerances.
  \emph{Status:} measured, with no acceptance tolerance stated. On smoke runs the maximum absolute log-probability difference before the first update is at most \EZeroDiffusionBeforeLargest{} on the diffusion paths, and the mean difference is \EZeroARBeforeMean{} on the autoregressive path (Table~\ref{tab:parity-smoke}); that gauge ran with a tolerance loose enough never to stop a run. On the diffusion paths both sides average a transition's log-density over the latent elements, so differences this small cannot separate a conditioning mismatch from numerical noise (Section~\ref{sec:fidelity-layout}). At the E1 geometry of 32 GPUs and 8,192 new tokens, four probes before the first formal run recorded \EOneGateChecks{} checks, \EOneGateBefore{} before a first update and \EOneGateAfter{} after a publication: the mean difference ranges from \EOneGateMeanMin{} to \EOneGateMeanMax{} and the largest single-token difference from \EOneGateTokenMin{} to \EOneGateTokenMax{}. Those probes carried a tolerance of \EOneGateTolerance{} on the single-token maximum with enforcement off, and every check exceeded it; the experiment record reads the mean instead, for which no tolerance was set. All of these are magnitudes, not a pass against a criterion.
  \item \textbf{Publication parity:} repeat the parity check after each LoRA or full-weight publication path used in an experiment.
  \emph{Status:} measured after publication on five of six handlers in smoke runs, at most \EZeroDiffusionAfterLargest{} on the diffusion paths (Table~\ref{tab:parity-smoke}), and after every publication of the six formal E1 runs and the seed 11 rerun, \EOneParityChecks{} checks in total (Table~\ref{tab:parity-e1}). In those runs the mean difference per rollout has a median of \EOneParityMedianMin{} to \EOneParityMedianMax{} and is at most \EOneParityMeanLargest{}, while the largest single-token difference reaches \EOneParityTokenLargest{}. These runs recorded the gauge without a tolerance; set against the \EOneGateTolerance{} the probes carried, every single-token maximum exceeds it and every per-rollout mean is below it. No acceptance tolerance has been set for either statistic. No recipe in these experiments uses the sixth handler, \texttt{CheckpointWeightSync}.
  \item \textbf{Batch-version integrity:} verify that an optimizer batch never mixes behavior versions when the configured algorithm assumes one version; while the asynchronous and agentic paths (and synchronous runs using vLLM's native weight-transfer engine) advance $v_b$ at weight publication, mixed-version batches can arise at runtime only on the asynchronous path, where multiple behavior versions may be in flight across batches.
  \emph{Status:} enforced by the asynchronous assembler at the commit E0 audited. No dedicated run is archived, and no asynchronous result is reported from this bundle.
  \item \textbf{Boundary integrity:} checkpoint and evaluation begin only after the async manager is empty; resume restores the optimizer version and republishes it before new rollout.
  \emph{Status:} enforced in code at the commit E0 audited; no dedicated run is archived.
  \item \textbf{Work accounting:} compare generated tokens or pixels, denoising and replay steps, scored samples, and committed optimizer updates across systems.
  \emph{Status:} not shown. No cross-system count comparison is archived with these runs.
  \item \textbf{Structural preconditions:} validate unique root IDs, equal layer counts, parent-major grouping, shared-field equality, and behavior-version provenance before merging or propagating credit; check against an ID-joined reference.
  \emph{Status:} both grouping scopes match an ID-joined reference on synthetic fixtures (E4a) and on recorded rollouts of the composed run (E4~v2, Section~\ref{sec:rq3}). The E4 record reports that advantage computation rejects interleaved rows, while reward propagation does not detect them.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Internal validation and the checkpoints it selects.}
The protocol evaluates the 60 AIME 2024/2025 problems every ten rollouts, with 16 samples per problem under the training sampling settings, and admits a checkpoint chosen by that series only as validation-selected.
Table~\ref{tab:e1-validation} and Figure~\ref{fig:e1-training}a report it.
In every formal run validation accuracy is highest at or before rollout 400 and lower at rollout 800.
The three formal GRPO seeds end between \EOneValidGRPOEndMin{} and \EOneValidGRPOEndMax{}, against \EOneValidGRPOStartMin{} to \EOneValidGRPOStartMax{} before training; the three DRPO seeds end between \EOneValidDRPOEndMin{} and \EOneValidDRPOEndMax{}, below their highs of \EOneValidDRPOPeakMin{} to \EOneValidDRPOPeakMax{} and above the highest value any formal GRPO seed reached.
Among the four saved checkpoints of a formal run, validation selects rollout 200 in five runs and rollout 400 in DRPO seed 11.
For GRPO seeds 22 and 33 the selected checkpoints score \EOneMathGRPOEarlyTwentyTwo{} and \EOneMathGRPOEarlyThirtyThree{} on MATH-500, above the base, where their final checkpoints are below it; for the DRPO seeds they score \EOneMathDRPOSelectedEleven{}, \EOneMathDRPOSelectedTwentyTwo{}, and \EOneMathDRPOSelectedThirtyThree{}, each above the run's final checkpoint.
The registered endpoint remains the final checkpoint, and because the validation problems are the AIME problems, no AIME score is given for a selected checkpoint.
GRPO seed 11's two lost checkpoints are the two of its four with the higher validation accuracy, so Table~\ref{tab:rq1-ar} shows only the later part of that run and its validation-selected score cannot be recovered.
The seed 11 rerun does not recover it: it reads the same prompts in the same order, yet its validation accuracy is above the original's at every evaluation from rollout \EOneRerunAboveFrom{} to \EOneRerunAboveTo{}, reaching \EOneValidRerunFour{} at rollout 400 against \EOneValidGRPOElevenFour{}, and below it at every evaluation from \EOneRerunBelowFrom{} until its training ended, with \EOneValidRerunLast{} against \EOneValidGRPOElevenLast{} at rollout \EOneRerunLastEval{}.
In four of the six formal runs the highest validation accuracy falls between saved checkpoints, so the 200-rollout cadence did not save the weights at those points.

\paragraph{Training diagnostics.}
Figure~\ref{fig:e1-training} draws the diagnostics the protocol lists for E1.
In all three GRPO seeds the mean response length grows from \EOneLenGRPOEarlyMin{}--\EOneLenGRPOEarlyMax{} tokens over the first 100 rollouts to \EOneLenGRPOLateMin{}--\EOneLenGRPOLateMax{} over the last 100, the share of answers that reach the 8,192-token cap from \EOneCapGRPOEarlyMin{}--\EOneCapGRPOEarlyMax{} to \EOneCapGRPOLateMin{}--\EOneCapGRPOLateMax{}, and the share of prompt groups whose eight answers all receive the same reward, which contribute no gradient, from \EOneZeroGRPOEarlyMin{}--\EOneZeroGRPOEarlyMax{} to \EOneZeroGRPOLateMin{}--\EOneZeroGRPOLateMax{}.
The update-size diagnostics stay small throughout: over 25-rollout blocks the median approximate KL is at most \EOneKLMedianLargest{} and the median largest importance ratio at most \EOneRatioMedianLargest{} in any of the six formal runs, and no rollout of the formal GRPO runs clips more than \EOneClipLargest{} of its tokens.
Single rollouts spike above those medians, to a KL of \EOneKLLargest{} and a ratio of \EOneRatioLargest{}.
The GRPO degradation therefore coincides with longer responses, more truncation, and less within-group reward variance, and not with large ratios or clipping, which locates it without explaining it.
The DRPO seeds end with longer responses still (panel b), so response length alone does not separate the arms.
The loss and wall-clock series the protocol also lists are not exported.
